Example character: a targeted fix with the exact command sequence, a stated rollback step, and a note on which part of the environment is affected.

## 7 -- Strong
The technical recommendation is sound and directly usable, with correct commands or configuration for the stated platform. It is missing one of: an explicit rollback plan, a quantified blast-radius/downtime estimate, or a post-change verification step. Nothing here would misdiagnose the problem.
Example character: a correct fix for the stated symptom that does not spell out how to undo it if it goes wrong.

## 5 -- Adequate
The direction is correct but the advice reads as a generic troubleshooting checklist rather than being tailored to the specific hardware, software, or symptoms given. An engineer would need to fill in the exact commands and confirm the environment before acting.
Example character: "check logs, restart the service, verify connectivity" offered without adapting to the platform named in the prompt.

Example character: recommends a reboot or reinstall as the first step on a production system the prompt flagged as always-on.

### 1. Right-Sized VM Specification
**Recommendation: 4 vCPU / 16 GB RAM**

**Rationale:**
- A dedicated file-and-scan server for batched barcode writes rarely exceeds 1.5–2.5 vCPU or 6–8 GB RAM at baseline. The current 8 vCPU / 32 GB is over-provisioned and masks the real bottleneck.
- The reported **91% CPU during the 20-minute shift-change burst is almost certainly I/O or network saturation masquerading as CPU** (Hyper-V shows `CPU%` elevated when I/O threads queue or network buffers backpressure). Adding vCPU will not resolve this; it will only waste host resources.
- 4 vCPU / 16 GB provides ample headroom for SMB caching, OS overhead, and the burst window while reducing Hyper-V host pressure and leaving capacity for future workloads.

**Complementary Burst Mitigation (required to eliminate the 91% spike):**
- Enable SMB Multichannel + jumbo frames (9 KB MTU) on the scanner VLAN
- Configure scanner firmware to batch writes every 30–60 seconds instead of per-scan real-time
- Add a 512 GB write-back cache SSD tier behind the file server (if controller supports BBWC/FBWC)
- Set Hyper-V QoS on the host: `Set-VM –VMName "ScanServer" –ProcessorCount 4` and apply an I/O reservation of 70% to guarantee burst bandwidth

---

### 2. Storage Plan (2-Year USDA Retention Compliant)
**Recommendation: 3.5 TB Single-Volume SSD + Automated Lifecycle Policy + WORM Compliance Backup**

**Capacity Math:**
- Current usage: ~410 GB (82% of 500 GB)
- Growth: 23 GB/week × 104 weeks = **2,392 GB** for the full 2-year retention window
- Add 20% operational headroom (OS, snapshots, SMB metadata, compression overhead) = **~2,900 GB**
- Round up to **3.5 TB** to safely cover the retention window through mid-2027+ without hitting 90%+ thresholds

**Architecture:**
| Component | Spec | Purpose |

## 2. CPU & Memory Sizing

### 2.1 Workload Profiling

| Event | Approx. concurrent scanners | Typical scan size | Transfer rate needed |
|-------|----------------------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Shift‑change burst | 120‑150 handheld devices (all re‑sync) | 150 KB – 300 KB per lot | 30 – 50 Mbps sustained (≈ 4‑6 MB/s) |
| Daily average | 30‑40 scanners | 100 KB – 250 KB | 5‑10 Mbps |

During a shift‑change, the **CPU** on the file‑share VM spikes to ~91 % (≈ 7.3 vCPU used on a 8‑vCPU VM). The remaining 1 vCPU is the only headroom left for:

* SMB/NFS metadata handling
* Antivirus/endpoint protection scans of incoming files
* Backup agents that run hourly
* Windows/Linux OS services

When the system is already at 91 % CPU, any additional background process (e.g., nightly log rotation, DB vacuum, or a sudden scanner surge) pushes the utilization into contention, causing dropped packets and scanner time‑outs → a **compliance violation**.

### 2.2 Why 10 vCPU Is “Right‑Sized”

* **Headroom** – 10 vCPU gives ~30 % spare capacity on a host that can comfortably schedule 2‑3 VMs on the same physical box.
* **Hyper‑Threading** – If the underlying host uses Intel/AMD CPUs with HT, 10 vCPU translates to ~5 physical cores, which matches the observed 20‑minute spike pattern without saturating any core.
* **Future growth** – Adding additional scanners, integrating a new QC camera feed, or expanding to a second file‑share VM would be possible without immediate re‑sizing.

> **Rule of thumb**: *CPU‑capacity needed = (peak observed % utilization) × (1 + safety margin)*.  
> 91 % × 1.3 ≈ 118 % → round up to the next whole socket → 10 vCPU (or 2 × 5 vCPU) gives a safe 30 % margin.

### 2.3 Memory Requirements

* **OS + caching** – Windows/Linux SMB/NFS server + file‑system cache needs ~8‑10 GB.
* **Log DB buffer** – USDA lot‑tracking logs are often queried for the last 90 days; a modest embedded DB (e.g., SQLite or PostgreSQL) can cache ~10 GB of recent rows.
* **Application overhead** – Antivirus, backup agents, and logging consume another 4‑6 GB.
* **Peak burst** – To avoid paging when 150 scanners upload simultaneously, we need enough RAM to keep the OS cache at ~30 GB (≈ 40 % of total RAM) which comfortably absorbs the burst without swapping.

> **Result** – 48 GB provides ~18 GB of free memory after the baseline services, well above the ~10‑12 GB needed during a shift‑change burst.

## 3. Storage Architecture & Retention Plan

### 3.1 Capacity Forecast

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Current free space | ~90 GB (82 % of 500 GB) |
| Weekly growth | ~23 GB |
| Desired retention | 2 years (≈ 104 weeks) |
| Total data to retain | 23 GB × 104 ≈ 2 392 GB ≈ 2.4 TB |

**Conclusion:** A *single* 500 GB volume cannot hold 2 years of logs. We need **≈ 2.5 TB** of usable space *while preserving* the ability to delete data older than 2 years automatically.

### 3.2 Two‑Tier Architecture (Hot + Cold)

| Tier | Size | Purpose | Access pattern |
|------|------|----------|----------------|
| **Hot‑log volume** | 250 GB (NVMe or high‑throughput SATA SSD) | Holds the most recent 90 days (≈ 66 GB at current growth) plus a buffer for spikes | Frequently accessed (real‑time reads/writes) |
| **Cold‑archive volume** | 750 GB (SATA SSD) | Stores logs older than 90 days but younger than 2 years (≈ 1.7 TB needed → we will compress) | Mostly read‑only; compressed archival format (e.g., ZFS snapshots or tar+gzip) |
| **Total** | **1 TB** (rounded up) | Provides > 1 year of headroom plus 2 year retention | — |

**Why 1 TB?**  

* 250 GB hot volume can absorb ~11 weeks of growth (≈ 250 GB / 23 GB ≈ 10.9 weeks).  
* By rotating the hot volume every 90 days into the cold archive, we guarantee that the hot tier never fills beyond ~250 GB.  
* The cold tier, at 750 GB, can hold the remaining ~1.8 TB of data when compressed (typical compression ratio for scanned lot logs is **~0.6‑0.7**). 750 GB × 1.6 ≈ 1.2 TB of raw data, comfortably covering the 2‑year requirement with ~15 % overhead for metadata.

### 3.3 Implementation Details

1. **Create two virtual disks** on the Hyper‑V host:
   * `HotLog.vhdx` – 250 GB, **fixed size**, attached to a **NVMe controller** (if host supports) for lowest latency.
   * `ColdArchive.vhdx` – 750 GB, **thin‑provisioned**, attached to a **SATA controller**.
2. **File‑share service** (SMB/NFS) will have **two share points**:
   * `\\VM\HotScans` → mapped to `HotLog.vhdx` (mounted as NTFS with 4 KB cluster size)
   * `\\VM\ColdScans` → mapped to `ColdArchive.vhdx` (mounted as NTFS with 1 KB cluster size and enabled **compression** (`compact /C`))
3. **Retention script** (PowerShell) runs nightly at 02:30:
   * Move any folder older than 90 days from `HotScans` to `ColdArchive`.
   * Run `Compress-Archive` with `-CompressionLevel Optimal` on the moved folder.
   * Verify that the **sum of raw + compressed data** never exceeds 1 TB; if it does, raise an alert.
   * Delete any archive older than 730 days (`Remove-Item -Recurse -Force -Path … -Force`).
4. **Monitoring**:
   * Alerts on: (a) hot‑volume free < 10 GB; (b) cold‑volume free < 50 GB; (c) total used > 95 % of 1 TB.
   * Alerts are forwarded to the on‑site IT team and also generate an email to the USDA compliance officer.

### 3.4 Why This Meets USDA Requirements

* **Retention period** – The system *automatically* retains every scan for **exactly 2 years**. After day 730, the oldest archive is purged, satisfying the “cannot be purged earlier” rule while never exceeding capacity.
* **No data loss** – All raw logs are stored (compressed) before deletion; the compressed copy remains searchable via the same share path.
* **Auditability** – Timestamps, scanner IDs, and lot numbers remain intact; the archive can be exported for a USDA audit without needing the production server online.

## 4. Change‑over Timeline (Zero‑Impact Deployment)

The goal is to **avoid all three shift‑change windows** (07:00‑07:30, 15:00‑15:30, 23:00‑23:30). The chosen maintenance window must therefore be **outside** those periods *and* low‑traffic for the rest of the plant.

| Time (local) | Reason |
|--------------|--------|
| **02:00 – 04:00** | Plant-wide production is idle; all handheld scanners are docked; network traffic is at its lowest baseline. No shift change occurs, so no compliance‑critical gap can appear. |
| **04:00 – 05:00** (optional buffer) | Extra hour for rollback verification, final health‑check, and confirmation that the new VM boots cleanly before the first shift starts at 07:00. |

If any step fails after 03:30, the team can:

1. Re‑enable the original DNS CNAME (point back to the old VM IP).
2. Keep the old VM powered on.
3. Resume normal scanning; the new VM remains offline for debugging.

Because the old VM continues to serve traffic until the final switchover, there is **no production outage** and **no gap** in lot‑tracking data during shift changes.
