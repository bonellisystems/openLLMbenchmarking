# Cybersecurity -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Severity is triaged correctly against the actual impact described, response actions follow containment before eradication before recovery, and any CVE, CWE, or ATT&CK reference used is real and correctly applied (never invented). Evidence-preservation is called out before disruptive remediation, and breach-notification or compliance timelines are flagged when the scenario implies regulated or sensitive data. The answer is calibrated about what needs live forensics versus what can be assessed from the facts given.
Example character: a triaged action list that isolates the affected system first, preserves logs, then patches, with the real CVE cited only where it is genuinely relevant.

## 7 -- Strong
The response plan is sound and correctly ordered (containment before eradication), and is directly usable by a responder. References to standards or CVEs are generic rather than specific, or evidence-preservation/notification timing gets only a light mention. Severity is judged reasonably even if not formally scored.
Example character: a correct incident-response sequence that never names a specific CVE or ATT&CK technique even where the prompt supports one.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice reads as a generic security checklist (patch systems, enable MFA, monitor logs) that is directionally correct but not prioritized by the actual severity or tailored to the specific incident described.
Example character: a list of general hardening steps with no sense of which one matters most given the described exposure.

## 3 -- Weak
The response gets the ordering wrong (patches or removes an attacker's access before containing them), misjudges severity, or pads the answer with boilerplate ("enable a firewall") that does not engage the specific scenario.
Example character: jumps straight to "eradicate the malware" on an active intrusion without isolating the host first.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites a fabricated CVE, CWE, or CVSS score as if verified, or recommends actively dangerous steps (disable logging, do not isolate an active breach) as sound practice. Empty or off-task response.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Fabricated CVE, CWE, or CVSS identifiers presented as real.
- Eradication or patching recommended before containment on an active incident.
- Recommending disabling logging or otherwise destroying evidence.
- Ignoring implied breach-notification or regulatory timelines when sensitive data is involved.
- Treating every finding as equally critical with no severity differentiation.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Severity correctly triaged and prioritized against the described impact.
- Containment-before-eradication-before-recovery ordering respected.
- Real, correctly-applied CVE/CWE/ATT&CK references where relevant.
- Evidence-preservation explicitly called out.
- Notification or compliance timeline awareness when data exposure is implied.

## Phishing‑Response Run‑Book  
**For:** Level‑1 Help‑Desk Technician (no security background)  
**Scope:** 6 users that opened a malicious Word document (CVE‑2022‑30190) and had their Microsoft‑365 passwords harvested.  

---

### 1.  **Why a simple password reset is NOT enough**

| What the attacker did | What that means for us |
|-----------------------|------------------------|
| 1️⃣ Sent a phishing email that exploited a **zero‑day Office bug** and silently downloaded a credential‑stealing page. | The attacker never needed the user to click anything else – they just opened the document. |
| 2️⃣ From the harvested credentials they logged in **successfully from a foreign ASN** and **no MFA was triggered** (the log‑on was “pass‑through”). | The attacker already has an **active session** in the cloud that bypasses MFA. Simply resetting the password does **not** terminate that session or invalidate any authentication tokens that are still alive. |
| 3️⃣ Created **mail‑forwarding rules** that copy and delete any incoming mail containing the word *invoice*. | The rule is a **persistent back‑door** – even after the password is changed the rule will keep copying future messages (including any password‑reset or MFA emails) to an external address the attacker controls. |
| 4️⃣ No user interaction beyond opening the document was required. | The attack surface is already broad – the compromised accounts may have been used to **enumerate other mailboxes, create delegates, or export data**. |

**Bottom line:** Changing the password only stops *new* log‑ins with the old password. It does **not**:

* Kill the attacker’s existing cloud session or OAuth tokens.  
* Remove the malicious inbox‑forwarding rule (or any other hidden rule).  
* Verify that the attacker hasn’t already accessed or exfiltrated other mailboxes or created hidden delegates.  

Therefore, after a password reset we must **terminate sessions, force MFA, and audit the mailbox** for hidden forwarding, delegates, and data loss.

## 3.  Step‑by‑Step Walk‑Through (What to Say to the User)

1. **Notify the user** – “We’ve identified that your account was part of a phishing attack. We need to secure it right away.”  
2. **Ask the user to log out of all devices** (or instruct them to close all browsers and sign out of Outlook/Teams).  
3. **Perform steps 1‑3 above** (MFA enable, session sign‑out, password reset).  
4. **Tell the user**: “You will be prompted to set a new password the next time you sign in. After that, you’ll also be asked for a verification code on your phone (or authenticator app).”  
5. **Explain the rule clean‑up**: “We found a hidden forwarding rule that could have sent copies of your emails to an unknown address. We have removed it and checked for any others.”  
6. **Re‑assure**: “Your mailbox is now being monitored for any further suspicious forwarding.”  
7. **Close the ticket** only after confirming the user can log in successfully with the new password and MFA.

# Phishing Response Runbook: Follina & Credential Theft
**Target Audience:** Level-1 Helpdesk Technician (MSP Client Support)
**Threat Level:** Critical (Financial Fraud Risk + Active Endpoint Compromise)
**Client Context:** 60 Employees, No Dedicated Security Staff.

---

## 🛑 CRITICAL WARNING
**Do not proceed if you are unsure.** This is not a standard password reset ticket. The client's endpoints and financial accounts are compromised. Follow this runbook exactly. If a step fails, **escalate to your MSP Security/SOC Team immediately.**

---

## 1. Immediate Containment (Do This First)

**Objective:** Prevent further data loss and stop attackers from using the infected machines.

1.  **Isolate the Infected Endpoints**
    *   Instruct the 6 affected employees to **immediately disconnect** their computers from the network (unplug Ethernet cable AND turn off Wi-Fi).
    *   **Do not shut down** the computers yet (closing the OS might trigger malware encryption or destroy forensic evidence).
    *   **Do not attempt to clean the virus.** Your team cannot safely remove Follina malware.

2.  **Freeze Financial Access**
    *   Immediately lock the `accountspayable@` and `controller@` accounts.
    *   *Note:* Do not just reset passwords yet (see Section 2).

---

## 2. Why Password Reset Alone Will Fail (For Your Knowledge)

**You must understand this to explain to management why this incident is serious.**

A password reset is **insufficient** for three reasons based on the facts:

1.  **Endpoint Compromise (The Malware):** CVE-2022-30190 ("Follina") drops malware onto the *computer*, not just the account. If the user logs in again on the same infected machine, the malware can capture the *new* password before the attacker even needs to hack the cloud. The machine itself is a "hot" target.
2.  **Session Persistence:** The sign-in logs show successful logins without MFA. Attackers likely have an **active session token**. Resetting the password does not always invalidate existing session tokens immediately. We must explicitly revoke sessions to cut their access.
3.  **Hidden Persistence (The Rules):** The attackers have already created inbox rules to steal "invoice" emails. Resetting the password does not delete these rules. The attacker will keep stealing emails even if the password changes.

---

## 3. Account Remediation (Microsoft 365 Admin Center)

**Action:** Log into the **Microsoft 365 Admin Portal** (admin.microsoft.com).

1.  **Revoke Active Sessions**
    *   Go to **Users > Active Users**.
    *   Select the **6 affected users**.
    *   Click **Reset Password** (set to "Password expires immediately").
    *   **CRITICAL:** Check the box that says **"Require the user to change their password at next sign-in."**
    *   **CRITICAL:** Check the box that says **"Revoke all existing sessions"** (this forces the logout of the attackers' sessions).
    *   *Why:* This cuts the "unfamiliar ASN" access mentioned in the logs.

2.  **Force MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication)**
    *   Go to **Users > Active Users > Security Info**.
    *   For **ALL 6 users**, ensure MFA is **Enabled**.
    *   If they were not using MFA (explaining why the foreign logins worked), enable it now.
    *   *Why:* This ensures future logins require a phone/app approval, stopping the foreign country logins.

3.  **Audit Sign-In Logs**
    *   Go to **Security Center > Audit Logs** (or Identity > Sign-ins).
    *   Search for the **6 affected accounts** within the last 48 hours.
    *   Look for the "unfamiliar ASN" (Foreign Country).
    *   *Note:* You do not need to investigate the logs deeply; simply note the foreign locations and forward this data to your MSP Security Team for further analysis.

---

## 4. Mailbox Forensics (Check Beyond the Rules)

**Action:** Log into **Microsoft 365 Exchange Admin Center** (admin.exchange.microsoft.com).
*Focus on `accountspayable@` and `controller@`.*

**You must check the following, as attackers often hide things here:**

1.  **Inbox Rules (The Visible & Hidden)**
    *   Go to **Mail > Mailboxes**.
    *   Select the mailbox > Click **Rules**.
    *   **Check:** Look for the "invoice" rule. **Delete it.**
    *   **Check:** Look for rules with **"Hidden"** status. Attackers often hide rules to survive a password reset. Delete *all* suspicious rules.

2.  **Outgoing Message Rules (Auto-Forwarding)**
    *   Go to **Mail > Mailboxes** > Select mailbox > **Forwarding**.
    *   **Check:** Is there any automatic forwarding set to an external email address (e.g., Gmail, Yahoo)?
    *   *Why:* Attackers might be sending copies of *all* emails out, not just invoices.

3.  **Mailbox Permissions (Delegated Access)**
    *   Go to **Mail > Mailboxes** > Select mailbox > **Delegation**.
    *   **Check:** Is there any user (other than the intended owner) listed with **Full Access** or **Send As** permissions?
    *   **Action:** Remove any unauthorized users immediately.

4.  **Deleted Items Folder**
    *   Log into the mailbox via Outlook (web or desktop) or check the mailbox contents in the Admin Center.
    *   **Check:** Search the **Deleted Items** folder for messages containing "Invoice."
    *   *Why:* This confirms the scope of data stolen before you found the rules.

---

## 5. Endpoint Remediation (The Infected Computers)

**Action:** Coordinate with the client to replace/clean the 6 machines.

1.  **Do NOT Reconnect to Network:** The 6 computers must remain offline until cleaned.
2.  **Re-image the Machines:**
    *   Since Follina is a complex RCE vulnerability, standard antivirus scans often fail to clean the persistence mechanisms.
    *   **Best Practice:** Back up *only* the essential data (files, not software settings) to an offline drive. Re-format the hard drive and reinstall the Operating System.
    *   **Alternative:** If re-imaging isn't possible immediately, isolate them from the internet and run a specialized malware removal tool (if available from your MSP team).
3.  **Verify Cleanliness:** Ensure the machine is clean before reconnecting to the network.

---

## 6. Communication & Final Steps

1.  **Notify the Client:**

### Priority 2: Force Password Reset for All 6 Affected Users
*   **Action:** Use the M365 Admin Center (or your tenant's helpdesk tool) to force a password reset for all 6 identified employees.
*   **Note:** This is a necessary step, but per the section above, it is **not sufficient**.
*   **Escalation:** Notify your security team that a password reset has been applied to these accounts.

### Priority 3: Disable All Active Sessions (M365)
*   **Action:** Go to **Microsoft Purview > Security > Sign-in Logs**.
    *   Find all sign-in events for the 6 affected users.
    *   Look for the "Sessions" management feature.
    *   **Disable all active sessions** for these users.
*   **Why:** This kills any active attacker session tokens immediately, regardless of whether the password has been changed.

### Priority 4: Disable the 2 Compromised Service Mailboxes
*   **Action:** Disable `accountspayable@` and `controller@` immediately in the M365 Admin Center.
*   **Why:** These are the accounts where the attacker set up the invoice-forwarding rules. Disabling them prevents further data exfiltration while you investigate.

## PHASE 2: MAILBOX FORENSIC CHECKS (Beyond Inbox Rules)

You have already identified inbox rules on `accountspayable@` and `controller@`. However, the attacker may have done more. Perform the following checks on **each affected mailbox** (the 6 employees AND the 2 service accounts).

### 5. Check "Sent Items"
*   **Action:** Search for emails sent in the last 48 hours to **external** domains (especially unknown domains).
*   **Why:** The attacker may have used the stolen credentials to send sensitive documents (like parts lists or financial data) to themselves or to a command-and-control server.
*   **Red Flag:** Any email with a large attachment sent to an unknown address.

### 6. Check "Contacts"
*   **Action:** Look for any **newly added contacts** in the last 48 hours.
*   **Why:** Attackers often add contacts to build a list of future targets for further phishing campaigns.
*   **Red Flag:** Contacts added with generic names (e.g., "CEO", "Manager") or bulk additions.

### 7. Check "Calendar" (User & Shared Calendars)
*   **Action:** Check the **Calendar** of the affected users and the `accountspayable@` / `controller@` shared calendars.
*   **Why:** Attackers may have created fake meeting invites to invite the user to external systems or to access sensitive shared folders.
*   **Red Flag:** Meeting requests from unknown senders, or calendar events with external attendees added without user input.

### 8. Check "Deleted Items"
*   **Action:** Search the **Deleted Items** folder for bulk deletions in the last 48 hours.
*   **Why:** The attacker may be hiding evidence of the phishing emails or financial documents they accessed.
*   **Red Flag:** Hundreds of emails deleted at once, or deletion of specific financial records.

### 9. Check for "Mail Flow Rules" (Inbox Rules) on the 6 Employees
*   **Action:** In each of the 6 employees' mailboxes, check the **Mailbox Rules** section.
*   **Why:** The `accountspayable@` and `controller@` accounts had rules to hide invoices. It is possible the attacker set similar rules on the **user** mailboxes (e.g., forwarding all emails to an external address and deleting them).
*   **Red Flag:** Any rule that forwards messages to an external email address, or deletes messages based on specific keywords.

### 10. Check "Attachments" (Outbound)
*   **Action:** Search the **Outbox** or **Sent Items** for any outbound emails with attachments.
*   **Why:** If the Follina payload was modified, or if the user's computer was compromised, the user might have accidentally sent a malicious document to a colleague.
*   **Red Flag:** Any document (Word, PDF, Excel) sent to an external address.

### 11. Check "Sensitive Items" (If Available)
*   **Action:** If your tenant has Microsoft Purview **Information Protection**, check for emails that have been labeled as "Confidential" or "Internal" in the last 48 hours.
*   **Why:** Attackers often look for sensitive labels to identify high-value data to exfiltrate.
*   **Red Flag:** Any labeled emails sent to external addresses.

## PHASE 3: ERADICATION & RECOVERY

### Priority 5: Enforce MFA for ALL Accounts
*   **Action:** Ensure **Multi-Factor Authentication is enforced** for every user account, including the 6 affected employees and the 2 service accounts.
*   **Why:** The logs showed MFA was not triggered. If MFA is not enforced, the attacker can log in with a password alone. Even after the password reset, MFA must be re-enabled to prevent recurrence.
*   **Note:** If MFA is enforced but the attacker still logged in without triggering it, the attacker has a **compromised secondary device**. This requires a deeper security investigation.

### Priority 6: Patch All Endpoints (Follina / CVE-2022-30190)
*   **Action:** Coordinate with your vendor to ensure all 60 workstations have the **latest Microsoft Office updates installed**.
*   **Why:** CVE-2022-30190 has a specific patch. Until the patch is applied, any employee who opens a malicious Word document is at risk.
*   **Escalation:** This is an IT/Security task, not a helpdesk task. Do not attempt to patch servers yourself.

### Priority 7: Monitor for 30 Days
*   **Action:** Set up alerts on the 6 affected accounts and the 2 service accounts.
*   **Why:** The attacker may return with a new campaign. Monitor for:
    *   Logins from foreign ASNs.
    *   New inbox rules.
    *   Unusual sign-in times.
*   **Escalation:** Forward any new alerts immediately to your security team.
