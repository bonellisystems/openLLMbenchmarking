# Data Analytics -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Any SQL or analysis logic is correct -- right join type, correct aggregation grain, no double-counting -- and any chart or visualization recommendation avoids deceptive framing (no truncated axes, no cherry-picked ranges). Statistical claims are appropriately hedged (correlation is not stated as causation), and every finding is traceable to the specific numbers given in the prompt rather than invented.
Example character: a query with the correct join and grain explained, a chart recommendation with the axis explicitly kept honest, and a finding that cites the specific input numbers.

## 7 -- Strong
The core analysis is correct and the findings are usable, but there is one minor query or logic gap -- an edge case in a join, for instance -- or the chart-framing caveat is left unstated. Nothing here would silently corrupt the result.
Example character: a correct query with a sound finding that does not explicitly address how the chart avoids visual distortion.

## 5 -- Adequate
The analysis is directionally correct but the query has a plausible bug (wrong aggregation grain causing double-counting) or the chart recommendation is not checked for distortion before being suggested.
Example character: a query that looks reasonable but would double-count rows on a one-to-many join.

## 3 -- Weak
The SQL or logic has a clear correctness bug (wrong join type, missing GROUP BY column), correlation is stated as causation, or a trend is asserted that the given data does not support.
Example character: a query missing a needed GROUP BY that would silently produce wrong aggregates.

## 0 -- Unusable
Presents fabricated numbers as if they were query output, or recommends a chart design that is actively deceptive (truncated axis to exaggerate a trend) as best practice. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- SQL or logic bug that would silently double-count or drop rows (wrong join type, missing GROUP BY column).
- Fabricated numbers presented as if they came from the given dataset.
- Chart design that would visually mislead (truncated y-axis, cherry-picked time window) recommended without caveat.
- Correlation stated as causation.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Query or logic correct for the actual grain and join semantics needed.
- Chart or visualization choice avoids known distortion patterns.
- Statistical uncertainty or the correlation-versus-causation distinction called out explicitly.
- Findings traceable to the specific numbers given in the prompt.

### 3. Revenue Impact Analysis

We calculate the dollar impact of a **10% improvement** in ASP and Leads **independently**, holding all other variables constant.

**Current Revenue:** $4,200,000

#### Scenario A: 10% Improvement in ASP
*   **New ASP:** $\$12,000 \times 1.10 = \$13,200$
*   **Units Sold:** 350 (Fixed)
*   **New Revenue:** $350 \times \$13,200 = \$4,620,000$
*   **Dollar Impact:** $\$4,620,000 - \$4,200,000 =$ **+$420,000**

#### Scenario B: 10% Improvement in Leads
*   **New Leads:** $2,800 \times 1.10 = 3,080$
*   **Rates:** 25% and 50% (Fixed)
*   **New Units Sold:** $3,080 \times 0.25 \times 0.50 = 385$

*   **Dollar Impact:** $\$4,620,000 - \$4,200,000 =$ **+$420,000**

---

### 4. Comparison & Explanation

**Which lever produces the bigger revenue impact?**
**They are exactly the same (+$420,000).**

**Why?**
The revenue formula is **multiplicative**:
$$Revenue = Leads \times LeadRate \times CloseRate \times ASP$$

In a multiplicative model, a percentage change in *any single input factor* (ceteris paribus) results in the **same percentage change** in the output.
*   10% increase in Price $\rightarrow$ 10% increase in Revenue.
*   10% increase in Leads $\rightarrow$ 10% increase in Revenue.

Because the current Revenue is $4.2M, a 10% lift in any single lever yields exactly $420k in incremental revenue.

---

### 5. Strategic Recommendation for Leadership

Since the raw dollar impact is identical, **revenue impact size should not drive prioritization.** Leadership must prioritize based on **feasibility, cost-to-serve, and risk.**

**Recommended Priority Order:**

1.  **Top Priority: Quote-to-Close Rate (Conversion Efficiency)**
    *   **Reason:** This is pure efficiency. Improving conversion requires no extra ad spend (unlike Leads) and no risk of price resistance (unlike ASP). If the sales team can close 10% more deals without changing price or lead volume, **margin** increases significantly.
    *   **Action:** Invest in sales training, refine qualification criteria, or adjust incentive structures.

2.  **Second Priority: Leads (Volume)**
    *   **Reason:** While controllable, scaling leads requires increased marketing spend (CAC). It is linear and scalable, but it directly impacts the bottom line through cost, not just top line.
    *   **Action:** Optimize channel ROI before simply increasing budget.

3.  **Last Priority: ASP (Price)**
    *   **Reason:** In the Ag equipment sector, ASP is **mostly external**. OEMs typically dictate MSRP. Trying to raise ASP by 10% often requires waiting for new model releases or risking a drop in volume (price elasticity). It is the least "agile" lever for a sales team.
    *   **Action:** Focus on "mix" (selling more high-spec tractors vs. entry-level) rather than hard price hikes.

**Summary:** Do not chase the 10% ASP hike as a primary growth strategy. Focus on **Conversion Rates** to protect margin, and **Leads** to scale volume, as these are the true operational levers the team owns.

### 5. Prioritization Recommendation

**Do not prioritize based on revenue impact alone.** Since the dollar impact is identical, leadership must prioritize based on **risk, controllability, and sustainability**.

**Recommendation: Prioritize Leads and Conversion Efficiency over ASP.**

**Reasoning:**

1.  **External Risk vs. Internal Control:**
    *   **ASP is fragile.** Ag equipment markets are competitive and price-sensitive. A 10% ASP increase is often impossible without losing customers to competitors or waiting for a supply shortage. Pushing ASP via price hikes carries high risk of volume erosion if the model breaks (i.e., if you raise price, customers may not stay, violating the "hold ASP fixed" assumption in reality).
    *   **Leads are controllable.** Marketing can reliably increase lead volume through channel expansion, campaigns, and partnerships. It is an internal lever with predictable returns.

    *   Improving Leads increases the total pipeline. This gives Sales more optionality to close deals, cross-sell, and maintain relationships even if close rates fluctuate.
    *   ASP improvements usually require product changes or significant mix shifts, which take quarters to execute.

3.  **Diminishing Returns on ASP:**
    *   ASP is often a lagging indicator of value delivery. If you push ASP up without delivering proportional value, you damage long-term brand equity and farmer trust.
    *   Leads and Conversion rates represent the health of your engine. Optimizing these builds sustainable growth.

**Strategic Action Plan:**
*   **Immediate Focus:** Invest in **Leads** volume (Marketing) and **Lead-to-Quote** speed (Sales ops). These are high-ROI, low-risk levers.
*   **Medium Focus:** Improve **Quote-to-Close** via better financing offers, demo programs, and trade-in tools.

Instead of raising prices, incentivize sales to sell higher-margin models (e.g., precision ag packages) where the value proposition is clearer to farmers. **Summary:** The math says they're equal; the market says Leads are the smarter bet. Prioritize the engine (Leads/Conversion), and let ASP grow through value and mix, not price pressure.
