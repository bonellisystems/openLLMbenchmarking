# Data Analytics -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Any SQL or analysis logic is correct -- right join type, correct aggregation grain, no double-counting -- and any chart or visualization recommendation avoids deceptive framing (no truncated axes, no cherry-picked ranges). Statistical claims are appropriately hedged (correlation is not stated as causation), and every finding is traceable to the specific numbers given in the prompt rather than invented.
Example character: a query with the correct join and grain explained, a chart recommendation with the axis explicitly kept honest, and a finding that cites the specific input numbers.

## 7 -- Strong
The core analysis is correct and the findings are usable, but there is one minor query or logic gap -- an edge case in a join, for instance -- or the chart-framing caveat is left unstated. Nothing here would silently corrupt the result.
Example character: a correct query with a sound finding that does not explicitly address how the chart avoids visual distortion.

## 5 -- Adequate
The analysis is directionally correct but the query has a plausible bug (wrong aggregation grain causing double-counting) or the chart recommendation is not checked for distortion before being suggested.
Example character: a query that looks reasonable but would double-count rows on a one-to-many join.

## 3 -- Weak
The SQL or logic has a clear correctness bug (wrong join type, missing GROUP BY column), correlation is stated as causation, or a trend is asserted that the given data does not support.
Example character: a query missing a needed GROUP BY that would silently produce wrong aggregates.

## 0 -- Unusable
Presents fabricated numbers as if they were query output, or recommends a chart design that is actively deceptive (truncated axis to exaggerate a trend) as best practice. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- SQL or logic bug that would silently double-count or drop rows (wrong join type, missing GROUP BY column).
- Fabricated numbers presented as if they came from the given dataset.
- Chart design that would visually mislead (truncated y-axis, cherry-picked time window) recommended without caveat.
- Correlation stated as causation.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Query or logic correct for the actual grain and join semantics needed.
- Chart or visualization choice avoids known distortion patterns.
- Statistical uncertainty or the correlation-versus-causation distinction called out explicitly.
- Findings traceable to the specific numbers given in the prompt.


## Task

State which lever produces the bigger revenue impact, or whether they're the same, and explain
why -- and recommend how leadership should actually prioritize between levers given what you
found (hint: revenue impact size is not the only thing that should drive prioritization).

### 4. Comparison & Explanation

**Result:** Both levers produce the **exact same dollar revenue impact** (+$420,000).

$$ \text{Revenue} = \text{Leads} \times \text{L2Q} \times \text{Q2C} \times \text{ASP} $$

In a multiplicative tree, a proportional change in any single leaf node results in the same proportional change in the root. A 10% increase in any variable scales the entire result by 1.10. Therefore, mathematically, the revenue leverage is identical across all levers.

### 5. Prioritization Recommendation

Since the **Revenue Impact is identical**, leadership should not prioritize based on revenue growth alone. Instead, prioritization must be driven by **Profitability, Cost, Risk, and Constraints**.

#### Key Decision Factors
1.  **Profit Impact (Margin):**
    *   **ASP Improvement:** Increases revenue by $420k with **zero incremental COGS**. Assuming COGS is fixed per unit, this likely adds $420k (or close to it) directly to Gross Profit.
    *   **Leads Improvement:** Increases revenue by $420k, but generates **35 additional units** sold. This incurs COGS for 35 tractors and requires **Marketing Spend** to acquire those leads. The profit impact is $420k − COGS(35 units) − Marketing Cost.
    *   *Verdict:* ASP improvement almost certainly yields higher **profit impact** unless ASP increases trigger a volume drop (elasticity risk).

2.  **Cost to Achieve (ROI):**
    *   **ASP:** Marginal cost is low (policy change, training). ROI is typically very high.
    *   **Leads:** Requires increased ad spend. Priority depends on **CAC**. If CAC is high, the profit dilution may be significant.

3.  **Risk & Elasticity:**
    *   **ASP:** Risk of **churn or volume loss**. If raising prices by 10% causes the Quote-to-Close rate to drop, the "held fixed" assumption breaks, and revenue could fall.
    *   **Leads:** Risk of **quality dilution**. Volume increases may stress sales capacity or lead to lower conversion rates over time.

#### Recommendation
**Prioritize ASP improvements first, followed by targeted Lead acquisition.**

*   **Action 1: Optimize ASP.** Review discounting authority, bundle pricing, and value-based selling. The revenue impact is the same, but the profit contribution is superior because it carries no incremental COGS or acquisition cost. *Guardrail:* Monitor close rates to ensure price increases aren't destroying conversion.
*   **Action 2: Evaluate Lead ROI.** Only scale leads if the **Contribution Margin** (ASP − COGS − CAC) is positive and the sales team can absorb the volume. If CAC is high, improving Quote-to-Close or ASP delivers better returns on the same revenue dollars.

**Bottom Line:** The math shows revenue is equally sensitive to both, but **ASP is the higher-leverage lever for profitability.** Leadership should attack ASP to boost margin, then use Leads only to scale volume when the unit economics support it.

## 3. Leadership Prioritization Recommendation

Since the *mathematical* impact is identical, leadership should not prioritize based on revenue lift alone. They should prioritize based on **execution feasibility, risk, and sustainability.**

### Why Equal Impact ≠ Equal Priority

**1. Execution Speed & Time-to-Value**
*   **Leads (Marketing):** Generating a 10% lift in leads requires ramping up ad spend, hiring, or launching new campaigns. This often has a lag time (2–4 quarters).
*   **ASP (Sales/Pricing):** A pricing adjustment can be implemented immediately in a single quarter.

**2. Risk & Customer Churn**
*   **ASP:** Ag-equipment buyers are often price-sensitive or locked into competitive pricing by OEMs. A 10% price hike risks alienating customers, increasing churn, or driving them to competitors/used equipment markets. The 10% gain may not be sustainable if demand drops.
*   **Leads:** The risk of lead gen is primarily wasted budget. The downside is lower, but the upside depends on conversion rates holding steady.

**3. Team Alignment & Accountability**
*   **Leads:** Owned by Marketing. If Sales doesn't convert them (Quote-to-Close drops), the lead spend was wasted.
*   **ASP:** Owned by Sales/Pricing. If Marketing floods the zone with leads but Sales can't justify the price, ASP stays flat.

### Recommended Strategy

**Prioritize the Quote-to-Close Rate first, then Leads.**

Even though the prompt asks to compare ASP vs. Leads, the hidden lever in the tree that offers the highest *control* and *speed* is the **Quote-to-Close Rate (currently 50%)**.
*   **Why?** In a 50% close rate, there is massive room for improvement (e.g., better financing options, inventory availability, or negotiation training). A 10% lift here (to 55%) adds $210,000 in revenue and carries minimal risk to the customer.
*   **Second Priority:** Leads. Because marketing spend is the primary cost driver, optimizing the volume of inputs is a "safe" growth lever.
*   **Last Priority:** ASP. Because it introduces the most external risk and potential customer backlash, price should only be moved if inventory is scarce or demand is inelastic.

**Summary:** When math is equal, choose the lever with the **lowest risk, fastest execution, and highest ceiling.** In this tree, the conversion rates (internal funnel) are the highest-leverage, lowest-risk levers.

### 4. Comparison & Strategic Recommendation

The relationship is **multiplicative**.
$$\text{Revenue} = \text{Leads} \times \text{Conversion Rates} \times \text{ASP}$$
In any multiplicative function, increasing *any* single factor by $X\%$ (while holding others constant) results in the total output increasing by exactly $X\%$. Since 10% of $\$4.2M$ is $\$420k$, both levers yield identical top-line revenue growth in isolation.

#### **How should leadership prioritize?**
Since the revenue impact is identical, prioritization should **not** be based on top-line growth alone. Leadership must prioritize based on **Profitability, Cost, and Feasibility.**

1.  **Profitability (Margin):**

    *   **Lead Improvement:** Increasing leads increases volume, but it also increases **Variable Costs** (e.g., marketing spend, sales commissions, logistics, warranty).

    *   *Winner:* **Leads.** It is more immediately actionable.

3.  **Risk:**
    *   **ASP:** Aggressive pricing to lower ASP increases risk of margin compression.
    *   **Leads:** Buying more leads without improving quality can dilute conversion rates (Leads $\uparrow$ but Quality $\downarrow$).

**Prioritize ASP/Quote-to-Close Efficiency first, then Leads.**
