## Anchors

The following anchors define what a 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 look like for this
business unit. Use them as the scoring scale for every answer.

# Operations -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The process redesign is tied concretely to the specific bottleneck and constraint stated in the prompt, with measurable KPIs that include a baseline and target, not just a goal restated as a metric. Implementation steps are sequenced with a clear first action, change-management or rollout risk is addressed, and resourcing constraints given in the prompt are respected rather than assumed away.
Example character: a redesign that names the actual bottleneck, gives a baseline-to-target KPI, and sequences the first three implementation steps.

## 7 -- Strong
The process improvement is sound and directly usable, correctly targeting the stated bottleneck. The KPI baseline or rollout sequencing is thinner than ideal, but the core recommendation would work as described.
Example character: a correct fix for the stated bottleneck with a named metric but no baseline value given.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic process-improvement guidance ("streamline the workflow," "reduce handoffs") that is directionally correct but not tied to the specific constraint described in the prompt.
Example character: general efficiency advice that would apply to almost any operational bottleneck.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer misidentifies the bottleneck, or proposes a process that ignores a stated hard constraint (headcount, budget, system limitation), with no measurable KPI anywhere in the response.
Example character: a redesign that requires more staff despite the prompt stating headcount is fixed.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites fabricated industry-benchmark figures as if real, or recommends a change that outright violates a stated hard constraint with no acknowledgment. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Fabricated industry-benchmark numbers presented as real data.
- Ignoring an explicitly stated resource or system constraint.
- No measurable KPI or success criterion anywhere in the response.
- Steps that restate goals rather than being actually sequenced and actionable.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Root cause or bottleneck correctly identified from the details given.
- Measurable KPI with a stated baseline and target.
- Rollout sequenced with a clear, concrete first step.
- Resourcing constraints from the prompt respected in the design.

**Chill Tumbler Math:**
*   **Cycle Definition:** One batch cycle (Load + Chill + Unload) = 20 minutes.
*   **Cycle Output:** 1,000 units.
*   **Effective Rate:** $1,000 \text{ units} / 20 \text{ minutes} = \mathbf{50 \text{ units/min}}$.
*   **Trigger Logic:** The tumbler accumulates 900 units ($900 \div 50 \text{ min feed} = 18 \text{ minutes}$) before the 20-minute cycle begins. In steady state, the input (50 u/min) matches the output (50 u/min) over the full cycle time.

### 2. Why the Chill Station Is NOT the Bottleneck

The floor supervisors are observing a **Work-In-Process (WIP) illusion**, not a throughput constraint.

*   **The Visual Queue:** During the 18-minute accumulation phase, the Evisceration station (continuous) dumps 900 units into the chill in-feed zone while the tumbler sits idle waiting to fill the batch. This creates a massive, visible pile-up.
*   **The Throughput Reality:** Once the cycle begins, the tumbler outputs 1,000 units every 20 minutes. This is a rate of **50 units/min**.
*   **The Comparison:** The station feeding the chill (Evisceration) runs at exactly **50 units/min**. Since the Chill's effective output rate (50) equals the input rate (50), the Chill station is **not slower** than the station before it.
*   **The Cause of the Queue:** The queue exists solely because the system requires a buffer of 900 units to trigger the next cycle. It is a synchronization artifact, not a capacity deficit.

### 3. The TRUE Line-Limiting Bottleneck

**The true bottleneck is Station 3: Portioning/Cut-up (38 units/min).**

*   **Reasoning:** In a line of stations in series, the overall system output is determined by the **slowest** station.
*   **Portioning (38)** is slower than Evisceration (50), Chill (50), and Packaging (42).
*   Because Portioning is the slowest link, it drags the entire line down to its speed. All stations upstream (Evisceration, Chill) are "starved" of downstream demand, causing WIP to accumulate *behind* Portioning (though the visual queue is currently most visible at the Chill due to the batch trigger mechanic).

### 4. Daily Output Analysis

    $$38 \text{ units/min} \times 480 \text{ min} = \mathbf{18,240 \text{ units}}$$

*   **Shortfall:**
    $$20,000 - 18,240 = \mathbf{1,760 \text{ units}}$$

### 5. Required Rate to Hit Target

To reach the 20,000-unit target, Portioning must run fast enough to produce that volume in 480 minutes.

$$\text{Required Rate} = \frac{20,000 \text{ units}}{480 \text{ minutes}} = \mathbf{41.67 \text{ units/min}}$$

Portioning needs to increase its rate from **38 units/min to at least 41.67 units/min** (a ~10% increase) to eliminate the shortfall.

*   **Hypothetical Scenario (2 Chillers):** Adding a second tumbler would theoretically double the Chill capacity to 100 units/min. However, the output past the Chill station is still limited by Portioning (38 units/min).
*   **Result:**
    *   Max Daily Output with 2 Chillers: **18,240 units** (unchanged).
    *   The extra chilled units will simply pile up at the Portioning station, creating a *new* WIP queue behind the portioner, rather than increasing sales.
*   **Conclusion:** Capital spent on a second tumbler is wasted because the constraint is downstream (Portioning), not upstream (Chill). To fix the output, you must invest in **Portioning**.
