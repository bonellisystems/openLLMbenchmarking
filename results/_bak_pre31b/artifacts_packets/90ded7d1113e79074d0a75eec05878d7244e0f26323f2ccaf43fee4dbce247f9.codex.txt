## 10 -- Frontier
Technical recommendations are correct for the stated platform (canonical tags, redirects, schema, robots directives used properly), keyword strategy is matched to actual search intent, and every recommendation is prioritized by expected impact versus effort. No black-hat tactic is suggested, and the answer is calibrated about ranking-factor uncertainty rather than asserting exact algorithm weights as fact.
Example character: a prioritized fix list starting with the highest-impact technical issue, each item tied to a specific, correctly-described mechanism.

## 7 -- Strong
The technical guidance is sound and usable for the stated platform, and keyword direction matches intent. It is missing an explicit effort-versus-impact prioritization, or one recommendation is generic rather than platform-specific, but nothing here is technically wrong.
Example character: correct on-page and technical fixes listed without ranking them by which matters most.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is SEO-fundamentals-correct (use target keywords, build backlinks, improve page speed) but reads as a generic checklist not tied to the specifics of the site or platform given.
Example character: a standard keyword-and-backlink checklist with no reference to the site's actual issues.

## 3 -- Weak
Contains outdated or incorrect technical claims (keyword stuffing helps rankings, more backlinks always help regardless of quality), or recommends tactics unrelated to the actual ask.
Example character: advice built around an outdated ranking factor treated as still authoritative.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends black-hat or manipulative tactics (cloaking, link farms, hidden text, doorway pages) as safe practice with no penalty-risk warning, or fabricates specific ranking-factor claims as verified fact. Empty or off-task.

- Recommendations prioritized by effort versus expected impact.

## Diagnostic Plan: Organic Traffic Decline Post-Relaunch

### 1. Key Metrics & Timeline
*   **Baseline Organic Sessions:** 28,000/month (30 days pre-relaunch average).
*   **Post-Drop Organic Sessions:** 16,240/month.
*   **Traffic Decline:** 42% reduction.

*   **Window:** 5 weeks post-relaunch (approx. 35 days elapsed).

*   **GSC "Duplicate, [model] chose different canonical":** Increased from ~0 to 380 URLs.

---

### 2. Hypothesis Elimination

**Hypothesis A: Content-Quality Decline (Ruled Out)**
*   **Reasoning:** The content team confirms no pages were rewritten, removed, or materially altered. A 42% sudden drop in organic sessions is too sharp for a gradual content decay scenario, which typically manifests over months. Furthermore, [model] Search Console does not display a "Content Quality" or "Thin Content" penalty signal. If the content had degraded, we would expect to see a gradual decline or a specific "Low-value content" flag, neither of which is present.

**Hypothesis B: [model] Core Algorithm Update (Ruled Out)**
*   **Reasoning:** There is no publicly announced core update or "Helpful Content Update" (or similar) that aligns with this 30-day window. While algorithm updates can happen without notice, the correlation of the drop starting *exactly* at the moment of the CMS relaunch makes a coincidence virtually impossible. If an algorithm update were the primary driver, the traffic would likely not have recovered even if the technical issues were fixed, and the GSC errors would not have spiked simultaneously with the drop.

**Hypothesis C: Manual Action / Penalty (Ruled Out)**
*   **Reasoning:** [model] Search Console provides a dedicated "Manual Actions" report. The absence of any notice here definitively rules out a manual penalty. Additionally, manual penalties usually result in a sudden, total drop in visibility for affected queries rather than a partial drop correlated with a specific technical event.

---

### 3. Most Likely Root Cause
**Canonical Tag Malformation / Duplicate Content Crawl Budget Exhaustion caused by Template Change.**

The convergence of two specific GSC signals points to a technical rendering issue within the new CMS template:

1.  **"Duplicate, [model] chose different canonical than user" (380 URLs):** The new template likely generates dynamic URLs (e.g., via parameters like `?sort=`, `?category=`, `?id=`) or renders content that [model] identifies as duplicate to another URL. [model] is resolving the conflict by picking a different URL than the one you submitted, effectively hiding your intended canonical version.
2.  **"Crawled — Currently Not Indexed" (1,100 URLs):** This is the direct consequence of the canonical errors. When [model] detects a page is a duplicate, it marks it as "Crawled, not indexed." The jump from 40 to 1,100 indicates the template is likely generating hundreds of "soft" variants of existing pages that [model] crawls but refuses to index because they are identical to higher-priority pages.

**Why this causes the drop:** The new template likely consumes a disproportionate amount of [model]'s crawl budget on these duplicate variants, leaving fewer crawl attempts for the high-value service pages. Additionally, users arriving at the "wrong" canonical URL (chosen by [model]) may encounter a non-optimized landing page or a redirect, leading to higher bounce rates and lower ranking signals.

---

### 4. Diagnostic Steps (Ordered Priority)

**Step 1: Audit the Canonical Tag Implementation in the New Template**
*   **Action:** Inspect the HTML source code of the high-traffic service pages in the live environment. Verify that the `<link rel="canonical">` tag is present, points to the correct absolute URL, and is not being overridden by JavaScript or the CMS default behavior.
*   **Why:** This confirms whether the template is accidentally injecting duplicate canonical tags or stripping the canonical tag entirely, forcing [model] to choose its own version.

**Step 2: Analyze URL Parameter Configuration in Search Console**
*   **Action:** Navigate to Search Console > URL Parameters. Review the new parameters introduced by the CMS (e.g., session IDs, sorting, filtering). Add parameters that are non-essential to [model] as "Ignored" or "No effect on URL."
*   **Why:** If the new template adds parameters that create 380 unique URLs for the same content, [model] will treat them as duplicates. Explicitly telling [model] to ignore these parameters will resolve the canonical conflict and the subsequent "Crawled, not indexed" errors.

**Step 3: Compare Sitemap.xml Before and After Relaunch**
*   **Action:** Pull the pre-relaunch and post-relaunch sitemap submissions. Identify which URLs present in the old sitemap are missing, or conversely, which new URLs appear that are unnecessary duplicates.
*   **Why:** If the new sitemap contains thousands of duplicate variant URLs, it is actively wasting crawl budget. Cleaning up the sitemap to only include canonical, unique URLs will help [model] prioritize the correct pages.

**Step 4: Check for `noindex` Meta Tag Injection**
*   **Action:** Inspect the rendered HTML of the 1,100 "Crawled — currently not indexed" pages. Search for `<meta name="robots" content="noindex">`.
*   **Why:** Some CMS templates include `noindex` in a default layout wrapper to prevent staging sites from indexing. If the new template accidentally added a global `noindex` tag to the live site, [model] would crawl the pages but refuse to index them.

**Step 5: Validate Internal Linking and Hreflang/Structured Data**
*   **Action:** Use a crawler (e.g., Screaming Frog or Sitebulb) to crawl the site and check for broken internal links, orphaned pages, or missing `hreflang` tags if the site serves multiple languages.
*   **Why:** The relaunch changed the "site structure." If navigation menus or breadcrumbs are broken, Googlebot cannot discover high-value pages, leading to a loss of indexation. This is a secondary check to confirm the primary canonical issue.

**Step 6: Monitor Crawl Stats (Robots.txt & Crawl Rate)**
*   **Action:** Check Search Console > Crawl Stats. Look for a drop in "Crawl Rate" or "Crawl Requests" immediately following the spike in duplicates.
*   **Why:** This confirms if the duplicates are consuming crawl budget. If [model] is spending 80% of its budget on 380 duplicate variants, high-value pages are being starved of crawls, which directly impacts indexing and ranking.

### Summary
The data indicates the CMS relaunch introduced structural duplication that [model] is flagging as canonical errors, leading to mass suppression ("Crawled — not indexed"). The immediate fix requires auditing the new template's canonical tag injection and configuring URL parameters in Search Console to eliminate the 380 duplicate variants.

### Why canonical tags matter

* Each page should declare **one unique canonical** that points to itself (or the preferred URL).
* If the tag points to a different page, [model] will index that canonical instead and treat the current page as a duplicate.
* Multiple pages pointing to the same canonical can lead to **duplicate content** penalties and a sharp drop in indexed pages.

## 3. First‑Order Checklist to Confirm the Root Cause

1. **Sample the “Duplicate, [model] chose different canonical” URLs**  
   * Use the **Coverage Report → “Duplicate (canonical is …)”** filter.  
   * Pick 5–10 representative URLs across the site’s hierarchy.

2. **Inspect the on‑page markup for each sampled URL**  
   * Open the page in a browser or use a crawler (Screaming Frog, Sitebulb).  
   * Verify the `<link rel="canonical" href="…">` tag.  
   * Check that it points to the **same URL** (or a correct canonical) and that the URL is live and crawlable.

3. **Check for accidental `noindex` or `nofollow`**  
   * Look for `<meta name="robots" content="noindex, follow">` or `noindex` in the header.  
   * Verify that the CMS template didn’t automatically inject a `noindex` on all pages.

4. **Validate the robots.txt file**  
   * Ensure no new disallow directives were added that block large sections of the site.  
   * Run the **Robots Testing Tool** on the affected URLs to confirm they’re crawlable.

5. **Audit the sitemap**  
   * Download the XML sitemap (usually `sitemap.xml`).  
   * Confirm that every URL that should be indexed is present.  
   * Run the sitemap through [model] Search Console’s “Test Sitemap” to catch errors or missing URLs.

6. **Use the URL Inspection Tool**  
   * Input one of the affected URLs.  
   * Check the “Indexing” status, “Canonical” line, and any “Crawl Errors”.  
   * Compare the canonical shown by [model] vs. the one declared on the page.

7. **Look for redirect or HTTP status anomalies**  
   * Use a crawler or the “Redirect checker” in Screaming Frog to ensure the URLs return **200 OK** and not **301/302** to unrelated pages.  
   * Verify that no 500‑level server errors are being returned during crawling.

8. **Review CMS template changes**  
   * Pull the version diff of the template before vs. after the redesign.  
   * Search for lines that add/modify canonical tags, meta robots, or any header injections.  
   * Check that the canonical logic correctly uses the current page’s URL, not a hard‑coded canonical.

9. **Cross‑check with Search Console Coverage Report**  
   * After the above fixes, wait 24–48 h and check if the “Duplicate (canonical is …)” count drops.  
   * Also monitor the “Crawled – currently not indexed” count for a corresponding decline.

## 3. Most Likely Technical Root Cause  

The **canonical‑related indexation explosion** is the strongest clue:

| Symptom | What it usually means |
|---------|-----------------------|
| **“Crawled – currently not indexed” → 1,100 URLs** | [model] crawled many URLs but decided *not* to index them, often because the page lacks a clear, authoritative canonical or has duplicate‑content signals. |
| **“Duplicate, [model] chose different canonical than user” → 380 URLs** | [model] detected conflicting canonical tags (or missing/incorrect ones) and **overrode** the publisher’s choice, treating the page as duplicate of another URL. This leads to **de‑indexation** of the original URLs and can cause a **dramatic loss of organic visibility** if those URLs were the primary traffic drivers. |
| **Traffic drop aligns precisely with launch** | The redesign most likely introduced or altered canonical tags, `robots.txt` directives, or URL parameters that unintentionally **blocked indexing** or **created duplicate‑content loops**. |

**Hypothesis:** The redesign moved content onto a new template that either:

1. **Removed or altered canonical tags** (e.g., missing `<link rel="canonical">`, self‑referencing incorrectly, or pointing to a pagination page), causing [model] to view many pages as duplicates of other URLs.
2. **Introduced URL parameters** (e.g., `?src=web`, `?mobile=1`, `?ref=...`) that were **not whitelisted** in `robots.txt` or the new CMS’s URL‑handling logic, creating “infinite” URL variations that [model] crawled but decided not to index.
3. **Changed the site’s architecture** (e.g., collapsed a top‑nav taxonomy into a single “landing page” that aggregates many former sub‑pages), causing **canonical conflicts** across hundreds of URLs.
4. **Accidentally added `noindex` or `nofollow` directives** (via meta tags or HTTP headers) on key landing pages due to a template oversight.

All of these would cause [model] to **drop those pages from the index**, directly reducing the pool of pages that can rank and drive traffic—hence the 42 % session decline.
