## Anchors

# HR & People Ops -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The recommended policy or action is consistent with generally-accepted employment-law caution -- at-will limits, documentation requirements, protected-class awareness -- without asserting a jurisdiction-specific statute as universal fact. Documentation or paper-trail steps are recommended where relevant (for example, performance history before termination), employment-counsel review is flagged for high-stakes actions, and the tone stays confidential and non-discriminatory throughout.
Example character: guidance that recommends documenting the performance issue first, names the point where legal review is needed, and never treats a protected characteristic as relevant to the decision.

## 7 -- Strong
The guidance is sound and usable, with the correct documentation instinct present. The counsel-review caveat is missing or generic in a scenario that is clearly higher-stakes (termination, accommodation, investigation), but nothing here is discriminatory or procedurally unsafe.
Example character: correct documentation-first guidance for a termination scenario with no explicit flag to loop in employment counsel.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic HR-fundamentals guidance ("be fair," "document everything") not tied to the specific personnel situation given, and any caveat present is perfunctory.
Example character: general fairness advice that does not engage the specific facts of the situation described.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer recommends an action that skips a required documentation or process step, treats a jurisdiction-specific rule as universally applicable without caveat, or carries a judgmental tone toward a protected characteristic implied in the prompt.
Example character: recommends termination without any reference to a documented performance record when the prompt implies none exists.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends an action that is facially discriminatory or clearly unlawful (for example, tying an adverse action to a stated protected characteristic) as if it were fine, or cites a fabricated employment-law statute. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Adverse action tied to a protected characteristic, or recommended with no documented performance record when the scenario implies neither exists.
- Fabricated employment-statute or case citation.
- No counsel or legal-review flag on a high-stakes action (termination, accommodation, investigation).
- Treating one jurisdiction's rule as universally applicable without caveat.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Documentation or paper-trail step recommended before any adverse action.
- Protected-class and accommodation awareness woven into the reasoning, not bolted on.
- Explicit flag for employment-counsel review on high-stakes actions.
- Confidentiality maintained throughout the response.

### 2.2 What **can** be promised  

| Promise | Rationale |
|---------|-----------|
| **Confidential handling of the complaint’s contents** | The HR Office will limit disclosure to those who must know in order to conduct a thorough, impartial investigation (investigator, legal counsel, senior manager of the accused supervisor). |
| **No retaliation** | County policy expressly forbids retaliation; any retaliation will be treated as a separate violation and addressed swiftly. |
| **Limited disclosure of the complainant’s identity** | Only the investigator and those required for protective actions will know the complainant’s name. No “need‑to‑know” personnel (e.g., other staff, the accused supervisor’s peers) will be told. |
| **Written updates** (as appropriate) | The complainant will receive status updates at key milestones (e.g., after evidence collection, after interviews, when a decision is reached). |

### 2.3 What **cannot** be promised  

| Limitation | Reason |
|------------|--------|
| **Absolute secrecy from the accused supervisor** | The supervisor must be informed that an investigation concerning them has been opened, otherwise the investigation could be deemed a sham and would violate due‑process rights. |
| **No record‑keeping** | All investigations must be documented and retained per County Records Management Policy and legal hold requirements. |
| **No disclosure to senior leadership** | The County Manager and/or the Board of Supervisors may need to be briefed on the status of a serious harassment claim, especially if interim measures affect operations. |
| **No guarantee of outcome** | The investigation must be fact‑based; confidentiality does not influence the factual findings or any remedial action. |

## 4.  INTERVIEW SEQUENCE  

**Goal:** Start with the least potentially biased source, gather the narrative, then corroborate with witnesses and documentary evidence. All interviews will be conducted **separately**, **in a private setting**, and **recorded (audio or detailed written notes)** with the interviewee’s consent.  

| Order | Interviewee | Rationale | Approx. Time |
|-------|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| **1** | **Complainant** (Employee) | Obtain first‑hand account while details are fresh; establish timeline, specific remarks, dates, and any witnesses to the supervisor’s comments. | 60‑90 min |
| **2** | **Accused Supervisor** (Direct Supervisor) | Provide the supervisor an opportunity to respond before other witnesses are interviewed; reduces perception of “gotcha” and helps assess credibility. | 45‑60 min |
| **3** | **Supervisor’s Manager** (e.g., Division Chief) | Determine if the supervisor has a pattern of similar behavior; assess knowledge of the reassignment decision. | 30‑45 min |
| **4** | **Project Team Members** (2‑3 staff who worked on the vendor‑logistics project) | Verify the reassignment timeline, any comments made about the complainant’s pregnancy, and overall project impact. | 30‑45 min each |
| **5** | **HR Business Partner** (or HR staff who processed the reassignment) | Confirm procedural steps taken for the project reassignment and whether pregnancy was a factor. | 30‑45 min |

### Final Note on Confidentiality  

While we will **shield the identity of the complainant** from all parties **except** those who must know to conduct a fair investigation (the investigator, legal counsel, and the accused supervisor’s immediate manager), absolute secrecy is **not feasible**. The County has a legal and ethical duty to:

1. **Inform the accused supervisor** that an investigation involving them has been opened (due‑process).  
2. **Preserve evidence** that may be subject to discovery in any future litigation.  
3. **Ensure safety and non‑retaliation**, which sometimes requires notifying senior leadership.  

All disclosures will be **limited to the minimum necessary information** and will be **strictly controlled**. Anyone who receives any part of the investigation will be required to sign a confidentiality acknowledgment and will be reminded of the anti‑retaliation provisions.  

By following the plan above, the County will meet its statutory obligations, protect the employee from further harm, and preserve the integrity of the fact‑finding process.

### Investigation Plan: Pregnancy Discrimination & Retaliation Complaint

**Subject:** Alleged pregnancy discrimination, hostile comments, and adverse action regarding project reassignment.  
**Complainant:** Employee (Direct report to alleged respondent).  
**Respondent:** Direct Supervisor.

---

### I. Response to Confidentiality Request
**This is the critical conversation. You must manage expectations immediately.**

**What to tell the employee:**
> "I hear you, and I appreciate you sharing this. Your safety and career are our priority. I want to be completely honest about what we can and cannot promise regarding confidentiality, so you can make an informed decision about moving forward.
>
> **What we CAN promise:**
> *   **Need-to-Know Basis:** We will restrict access to this investigation to the absolute minimum number of people necessary to conduct a fair inquiry.
> *   **Anti-Retaliation:** You will face zero retaliation. We have a zero-tolerance policy for retaliation, and we will take specific steps to protect you. If your supervisor or anyone else penalizes you for filing this, we will address that as a separate, serious violation.
> *   **Anonymity in Reporting:** If we write a summary or recommendation, your name may be redacted or replaced with initials, depending on the outcome.
> *   **Support:** We will check in with you regularly and can adjust work conditions to reduce stress during this process.
>
> **What we CANNOT promise:**
> *   **100% Secrecy:** We cannot guarantee your supervisor will never know. Because this complaint involves interactions between you and your supervisor, and specifically concerns a project they reassigned, if we substantiate the complaint, we are legally and ethically required to inform the supervisor that their actions are being reviewed and corrective action is being taken. They will likely deduce that you filed the complaint.
> *   **Due Process for the Respondent:** Your supervisor has employment rights. If allegations are substantiated, they must be informed of the specific allegations so they can respond. We cannot investigate a person in secret.
> *   **Identity Disclosure Risks:** If we interview the colleague who received the project, or others who may have heard the comments, there is a risk the supervisor may deduce the source based on the context of the questions.
>
> **Why these limits exist:**
> 1.  **Legal Liability:** If we suppress the investigation and later you are terminated or harassed, the county could be liable for failing to act on a known complaint, or for allowing retaliation to continue.
> 2.  **Investigative Integrity:** An investigation requires gathering facts. If we hide the source completely, the investigation may be compromised, or we may be unable to take corrective action, which could allow discriminatory behavior to continue.
>
> **Our Recommendation:**
> We can proceed with measures to maximize your privacy. We will use the **least intrusive method** possible to gather evidence. However, if you file this, you must accept that your supervisor will likely learn of it. If you choose not to file, you forego the county's ability to protect you and correct the behavior. This decision is yours."

---

### II. Impartial Investigator Selection
Given the sensitivity and the complainant's fear, the investigator must be selected carefully.

*   **Criteria:**
    *   **No Prior Relationship:** Must have no prior management interaction with either party.
    *   **Seniority:** Must be senior enough to recommend corrective action against a supervisor without fear of pushback.
    *   **Training:** Certified in employment law, PDA/Title VII, and investigative best practices.
    *   **Trust:** Must be viewed as neutral by the complainant.
*   **Recommended Selection:**
    *   **Option A (External Consultant):** Given the "100% confidential" request and fear of retaliation, an external HR consultant provides the highest level of perceived impartiality. They can be contracted to perform the investigation and report findings to County Council or a designated executive without embedding in the local chain of command.
    *   **Option B (Internal, if external not feasible):** A **Director-level HR Generalist** or **HR Business Partner** from a *different* division/department, reporting to the County Administrator or Human Resources Director, bypassing the supervisor's chain of command.
    *   **Conflict Check:** Both options require a documented conflict-of-interest check confirming no personal or professional bias.

---

### III. Interim Measures to Protect the Employee
These measures go into effect **immediately upon acceptance of the complaint**, before interviews begin.

1.  **Reporting Line Suspension:** Temporarily reassign the supervisor's authority over the employee's daily work assignments, schedule, and performance inputs to a neutral interim lead (e.g., an HR liaison or another manager), while keeping the employee in their current role. This prevents the supervisor from making further adverse actions or creating a hostile environment during the probe.
2.  **Project Status Clarification:** Place the county-fair vendor-logistics project in a "frozen" status regarding personnel changes pending investigation. The employee may be asked to remain available for consultation on the project, or HR may temporarily assist with oversight. *Note: Do not simply return the project immediately as this could be seen as interfering with business operations; instead, frame it as "HR is ensuring all staffing decisions are reviewed for compliance."*
3.  **Anti-Retaliation Notice:** Issue a written directive to the supervisor (and all department staff) emphasizing the prohibition on retaliation against anyone participating in an investigation or asserting rights. This creates a documented paper trail if retaliation occurs.
4.  **Wellness Check-Ins:** Schedule weekly private check-ins between the employee and the investigator (or HR liaison) to monitor well-being, assess workload, and ensure no retaliation is occurring.
5.  **Preservation of Evidence Notice:** Instruct relevant parties (including the supervisor) to preserve all emails, texts, calendars, and project files related to the employee and the project.

---

### IV. Interview Sequence
1.  **Complainant (Employee):**
    *   *Goal:* Establish facts, obtain specific dates/times/wording of comments, identify witnesses, explain the project reassignment context.
    *   *Approach:* Trauma-informed, open-ended questions. Obtain written statement. Ask: "Who else might have heard these comments?" and "Do you have any messages or records?"
2.  **Witnesses (Third Parties):**
    *   *Goal:* Corroborate comments or context.
    *   *Targets:* The colleague who received the project, other team members in the supervisor's vicinity, anyone the employee identifies.
    *   *Approach:* Interview witnesses *before* the supervisor to prevent coaching. Use non-leading questions.
3.  **Respondent (Supervisor):**
    *   *Goal:* Due process; allow the supervisor to respond to specific allegations.
    *   *Approach:* Present the allegations factually. "It has been alleged that on [dates] you made comments regarding pregnancy..." Ask for their explanation of the project reassignment rationale. *Note: The supervisor does not need to be told the employee's name if the investigation can be conducted via anonymous witness interviews and documentary evidence, but if the allegation is substantiated, the supervisor must eventually be informed of the source for corrective action.*
4.  **Project Colleague (Specific):**
    *   *Goal:* Understand the reassignment decision.
    *   *Approach:* Why was the project reassigned? What was the stated reason? Was the employee mentioned? What is the colleague's relationship with the supervisor?

### V. Evidence to Gather
**Documentary Evidence:**
*   **Project Records:** Emails/meetings regarding the county-fair project. Look for the timeline of reassignment relative to the pregnancy disclosure. Compare the stated reason for reassignment against the employee's workload/capacity data.
*   **Communication Logs:** Emails, Slack/Teams messages, and calendar invites between supervisor and employee around the pregnancy disclosure and project reassignment.
*   **HR Files:** Employee's performance reviews, attendance records, and any prior complaints against the supervisor.
*   **Policy Documents:** County Equal Employment Policy, Anti-Retaliation Policy, Pregnancy Accommodation Policy.
*   **Staffing Data:** Workload metrics to assess if the claim "can't handle workload" has a performance basis or is pretextual.

**Testimonial Evidence:**
*   **Witness Statements:** Written accounts from anyone who heard the comments ("When's your last day?").
*   **Colleague Statement:** Written account from the project replacement regarding the selection criteria and any pressure from the supervisor.

**Digital Forensics:**
*   Review supervisor's calendar for meetings with the employee where comments might have been made.
*   Check if the supervisor deleted messages (indicates consciousness of guilt).

---

### VI. Key Risks & Considerations
*   **Pretext:** The supervisor may claim the project was reassigned due to "availability" or "workload." The investigation must determine if this rationale applies equally to other pregnant or non-pregnant employees. If the supervisor has a pattern of reassigning projects for pregnant employees, this is strong evidence of discrimination.
*   **Hostile Work Environment:** The comments ("pop," workload doubts) may constitute harassment even without the project reassignment. The severity and frequency must be documented.
*   **Retaliation is Standalone:** Even if the pregnancy discrimination claim cannot be fully proven, if the supervisor retaliates during the investigation, the county is liable for that. The interim measures and monitoring are essential here.
*   **County Liability:** Failure to investigate promptly can create liability. The investigation should be documented as "expedited" given the nature of the allegations.

### VII. Conclusion & Reporting
*   The investigator will produce a written report with findings (Substantiated / Unsubstantiated / Inconclusive).
*   If **Substantiated**: Recommendations will include corrective action for the supervisor (up to and including termination), restoration of the project or other remedies for the employee, and training. The supervisor will be notified of the outcome.
*   The report will be shared with the County Administrator/Human Resources Director. The employee will be informed of the conclusion and any remedial steps taken, respecting the limits of confidentiality discussed in Section I.

**Immediate Next Step:** Schedule the meeting with the employee to discuss the confidentiality boundaries and obtain their informed consent to proceed.

- **Fairness**: The investigation must be impartial, thorough, and respect the rights of both the complainant and accused.  
- **Legal Compliance**: Adhere to the PDA and Title VII to protect the employee from discrimination/harassment.  
- **Protection**: Use interim measures to prevent further harm and retaliation.

## 5.  Responding to the Employee’s Confidentiality Request  

### 5.1  What the Employee Asked For  
> “Please keep this 100 % confidential — I don’t want ANYONE, including my supervisor, to know I filed this, or I’m afraid of what will happen to my career here.”

### 5.2  What the Law Allows  
| Legal Principle | Practical Implication |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Confidentiality vs. Investigation** | The agency must conduct a **fair, thorough investigation**. This often requires disclosing the complaint to the **alleged harasser** and to witnesses. Absolute secrecy is **not legally possible** without jeopardizing the investigation. |
| **Statutory Obligations** | Under Title VII and state anti‑discrimination statutes, employers are required to **investigate and remediate**. The investigation file is a **record** that may be subject to discovery if a lawsuit arises. |
| **Need‑to‑Know Principle** | Only individuals who have a **legitimate role** in the investigation (HR investigators, legal counsel, witnesses) may be told the details. All other staff must be kept out of the loop. |
| **Retaliation Prohibition** | Any action that **reveals** the complainant’s filing to others in a way that could cause retaliation is itself a violation of law. |

### 5.3  How to Communicate the Limits  
1. **Acknowledge the Request** – “We understand how important confidentiality is to you, and we will protect your privacy to the fullest extent allowed by law.”  
2. **Explain What Must Be Shared** – “In order to conduct a fair investigation, we must share the details of your complaint with the person you are reporting about, any witnesses we need to interview, and certain members of the HR team who need the information to manage the process.”  
3. **Set Clear Expectations** – “Only those individuals who are directly involved in the investigation will be told what you have shared. No one else — including your supervisor — will receive this information unless it is required by law or a court order.”  
4. **Document the Acknowledgment** – Have the employee sign a **confidentiality acknowledgment form** that records she was informed of the limits. Keep this form in the case file.  
5. **Re‑affirm Protection Against Retaliation** – “Any attempt by anyone to disclose the contents of this complaint to others, or to retaliate against you, is a violation of county policy and will be investigated and disciplined.”  

### 5.4  Sample Script for the Initial Conversation  

> *“Thank you for bringing this to our attention. We take allegations of pregnancy‑related discrimination very seriously, and we are committed to protecting your privacy. While we cannot guarantee that **no one** will ever learn of your complaint — because a thorough investigation requires sharing the information with a limited group of people who must know in order to complete the facts — we will only disclose it to those who have a direct role in the investigation (e.g., the investigator, legal counsel, and any witnesses). All of these individuals are under a legal duty to keep the matter confidential. We will also take steps to ensure that no one uses this information to treat you unfairly. If anyone tries to reveal your complaint to others or to retaliate against you, that will be treated as a separate violation and will be investigated immediately.”*  

### 5.5  What the Employee May Still Request  
- **Written confirmation** of the confidentiality safeguards.  
- **Assurance** that any disclosure will be limited to the smallest group possible.  
- **Escalation** to an external investigator if she believes internal confidentiality cannot be protected.  

*The investigator should note that if the employee continues to request absolute secrecy after being told the limits, the agency must still proceed with the investigation; refusal to cooperate may be considered in assessing the merits of the claim, but it cannot be used as a basis for retaliation.*

**Case Classification:** Potential violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (Pregnancy Discrimination), the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), and the County's Equal Employment Opportunity policy.

*   **The Complaint:** The original written letter (already secured).
*   **Project Records:** Timeline of the "vendor-logistics project," meeting minutes, email chains, and the official reassignment memo. Verify if the reassignment coincided with the disclosure of pregnancy.
*   **Performance History:** The employee's performance reviews, attendance records, and any prior informal feedback to establish a baseline (looking for regression or sudden change).
*   **HR Leave/Accommodation Files:** Any prior requests for leave, lactation breaks, or accommodations.
*   **Supervisor's File:** Performance reviews, prior complaints, and disciplinary records to check for patterns.

**Witness Identification:**
*   Identify colleagues who worked on the vendor-logistics project.
*   Identify anyone who may have overheard the verbal comments.
*   Identify any other employees who interacted with the supervisor regarding the pregnancy.

### 3. Interview Sequence

Interviews must be conducted individually, in private, and documented with verbatim notes.

**Phase 1: The Complainant (Employee)**
*   **Timing:** Conduct within 24–48 hours of receiving the complaint.
*   **Purpose:** Secure the employee's account while memories are fresh. This establishes the baseline facts without the presence of the accused, which reduces the risk of intimidation.
*   **Format:** Open-ended questions. Allow the employee to provide a full narrative. Do not lead or suggest answers.

**Phase 2: The Respondent (Supervisor)**
*   **Timing:** Conducted *before* interviewing witnesses.
*   **Purpose:** To give the supervisor a chance to explain their side while the evidence is still fresh. Interviewing witnesses first risks them being coached by the supervisor.
*   **Notice:** The supervisor must be informed that a formal investigation is underway against them regarding workplace conduct. (See Confidentiality Section for nuance).

**Phase 3: Witnesses**
*   **Timing:** After both the complainant and respondent have been interviewed.
*   **Purpose:** To corroborate or contradict statements.
*   **Protection:** Witnesses should be informed that their participation is voluntary and that retaliation against them is also prohibited.

**Phase 4: Review & Conclusion**
*   Analyze all statements against documentary evidence.
*   Determine if there is a pattern of behavior or isolated incidents.
*   Draft a written report with findings and recommendations for HR/Director review.

### 4. Interim Measures to Protect the Employee

While the investigation is pending, the County has an affirmative duty to prevent further harm and retaliation.

**A. Separation of Parties (Immediate)**
*   **Action:** Place a "No Contact" directive between the employee and the supervisor.
*   **Implementation:** If they share an office, relocate the employee to a different workspace. If they share a team, temporarily reassign the employee to a neutral role or provide remote work options (if available).
*   **Rationale:** Prevents the supervisor from making further verbal comments or influencing the employee's work environment.

**B. Retaliation Protection**
*   **Action:** Issue a verbal and written reminder to all staff (specifically the supervisor's direct reports) that retaliation for filing a complaint is a fireable offense.
*   **Action:** Monitor the employee's schedule, assignments, and interactions for any signs of retaliation (e.g., sudden schedule changes, negative reviews, exclusion from meetings).

**C. Light Duty / Accommodation**
*   **Action:** If the employee requests it, evaluate reasonable accommodations under the PWFA (e.g., more frequent breaks, reduced physical workload).
*   **Action:** Do not unilaterally lower her workload; accommodate her request rather than reassigning the work without consent.

**D. Timeline Monitoring**
*   **Action:** Set a strict deadline for the investigation to conclude (e.g., 30 days) and communicate this to the employee to reduce anxiety and demonstrate good faith.

### 5. Response to the Confidentiality Request

**The Core Promise:**
HR must respond to the employee with transparency and empathy. The following script/response structure is recommended:

> *"We take your request for confidentiality very seriously. We will protect your identity to the greatest extent possible and will limit who knows you have made this complaint. However, I need to be honest about what we can and cannot promise you."*

**What HR Can Promise:**
1.  **Identity Protection:** Your name and medical information will not be shared with coworkers, your supervisor, or other departments without a compelling legal or safety reason.
2.  **Process Privacy:** The investigation process will not be publicized. You will not be asked to participate in disciplinary meetings that could expose you.
3.  **Retaliation Ban:** If anyone retaliates against you because you filed this complaint, it is illegal and will result in disciplinary action. We will monitor this closely.

**What HR Cannot Promise (and Why):**
1.  **That the Supervisor Won't Be Told an Investigation Exists:**
    *   **Why:** Under federal employment law (Title VII) and due process, the accused party (the supervisor) has the right to know that a formal investigation is occurring so they can respond to the allegations. An investigation where the accused is kept in the dark is a "sham investigation" and could expose the County to further liability if findings are overturned in court.
    *   **Mitigation:** HR can inform the supervisor that "a formal complaint has been received regarding workplace conduct" without immediately disclosing the specific nature of the pregnancy claims until the investigation is complete or legally required. This balances the employee's safety with the supervisor's right to due process.

2.  **That No One Will Find Out:**
    *   **Why:** HR staff involved in the investigation (the investigator, the HR manager, the Director) will know. This is necessary to manage the investigation, document the process, and ensure the supervisor is held accountable.
    *   **Mitigation:** The number of people who know is minimized to only those directly involved in the investigation.

3.  **That the Supervisor Won't Be Disciplined:**
    *   **Why:** HR cannot promise a specific outcome regarding the supervisor's employment. We can only promise a fair process.
    *   **Mitigation:** The employee is informed that findings are based solely on evidence and will result in appropriate action.

**Why This Distinction Matters:**
If HR promises 100% confidentiality and cannot keep it, the County loses the trust of the complainant and potentially exposes itself to EEOC claims for failing to investigate properly. If HR *does* share the investigation with the supervisor, it is a necessary legal safeguard. The compromise is to delay the *specific details* to the supervisor until the investigation concludes, allowing the supervisor to be notified of the investigation generally without being told the employee fears pregnancy-based retaliation.

**Documentation of the Request:**
The employee's request for confidentiality must be recorded in the investigation file verbatim. This demonstrates that HR made a good-faith effort to protect the employee and that any disclosure was necessary for the investigation, not a willful breach of privacy.

**5. Responding to the Confidentiality Request**

**What Can Be Promised:**

- **Reasonable Confidentiality:** The county will take reasonable steps to protect the employee’s identity, including using pseudonyms in internal reports and restricting access to investigation documents.  

**What Cannot Be Promised (and Why):**
- **Absolute Anonymity:** *We cannot guarantee that the supervisor—or anyone else in the organization—will never learn who filed the complaint.*  
  - **Legal & Procedural Necessity:** Under federal (Title VII) and most state anti‑discrimination statutes, the employer must be able to investigate the allegations thoroughly. This often requires the complainant to provide specific details and may involve direct questioning of the alleged perpetrator, which inherently risks the complainant’s identity becoming known.  
  - **Due‑Process Rights:** The supervisor has a constitutional right to know the nature of the allegations against them and to respond. Withholding the identity entirely could impair a fair adjudication and expose the county to claims of an inadequate investigation.  
  - **Witness Cooperation:** Witnesses may be called to provide statements; they may infer the complainant’s identity based on context (e.g., “the pregnant employee leading the vendor‑logistics project”).  

**Transparent Communication with the Employee:**
> “We understand your strong desire for confidentiality and will take every reasonable step to limit knowledge of this complaint to the investigators and essential decision‑makers. However, full anonymity cannot be guaranteed because a thorough investigation—required by law and our policies—may necessitate sharing identifying information with the supervisor (or their representative) so they can respond, and possibly with witnesses who need to provide relevant facts. We will document all steps taken to protect your privacy and will keep you informed of any developments that could affect your confidentiality.”
