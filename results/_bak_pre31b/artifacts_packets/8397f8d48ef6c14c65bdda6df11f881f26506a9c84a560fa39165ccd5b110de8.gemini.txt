### **⚠️ Explicit Compliance Constraints: Claims to Avoid**

Given that clinical validation and FDA clearance are **pending/incomplete**, all content published during this pre-launch window must strictly adhere to the following restrictions. **No content may make the following claims:**

1.  **No Clinical Accuracy Claims:** Avoid statements regarding error rates, sensitivity, specificity, positive/negative predictive values, or correlation coefficients.
2.  **No Diagnostic Performance Claims:** Avoid claims that the test can detect, rule out, or monitor specific conditions or diseases.
3.  **No Medical Efficacy Claims:** Avoid claims that the test provides medical advice, diagnoses, cures, or treats any disease or disorder.
4.  **No FDA Clearance Claims:** Do not state the test is "FDA cleared," "FDA approved," or "FDA regulated." You may reference FDA guidelines generally, but cannot imply regulatory status for this product.
5.  **No Comparative Claims:** Avoid comparing accuracy, quality, or performance against competitors or existing tests.
6.  **No "Gold Standard" or "Clinical Grade" Claims:** Avoid terms that imply the product has met clinical validation requirements if it has not.

**Safe Language Guideline:**
*   *Use:* "Educational information," "Genetic markers associated with," "Explore your traits," "Understand your history," "Learn about potential risks."
*   *Avoid:* "Diagnoses," "Detects," "Accurate results," "Clinically validated," "Prevents," "Treats."

### Content Dos & Don’ts (Quick Reference)

**Do:**  
- Use **educational, explainer‑style** language.  
- Cite **peer‑reviewed studies**, **industry reports**, or **expert quotes** (e.g., geneticists, ethicists).  
- Highlight **benefits of genetic literacy** and **empowerment**.  
- Include **visual aids** (infographics, diagrams) that illustrate concepts, not test results.  
- End each post with a **call‑to‑action** that invites readers to **sign up for launch updates** or **explore related educational resources**.

**Don’t:**  
- State or imply **any quantitative performance metric** (sensitivity, specificity, predictive value).  
- Claim the test **diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents** any condition.  
- Compare the upcoming test to **cleared competitors** in a way that suggests superiority or equivalence.  
- Use language that could be interpreted as **medical advice** (e.g., “you should get this test”).  
- Present **patient‑journey anecdotes** as evidence of the test’s efficacy.

### Compliance Guardrails: Claims to Avoid
**Given that clinical validation and FDA clearance are pending, all 20 pieces must strictly avoid the following claims.** Marketing content must be reviewed against this list before publication:

## 3️⃣  Explicit Claim‑Avoidance Rules  

Because the new at‑home test **has not yet received FDA clearance** and its clinical validation is still pending, **all pre‑launch content must avoid any of the following**:

| Forbidden Claim Type | What to Avoid (examples) | Why It Matters |
|----------------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **Clinical Accuracy** | • “Our test is >99 % accurate for detecting BRCA1 mutations.” <br>• “You’ll get a definitive answer about your Alzheimer’s risk.” | Accuracy statements imply that the test has been clinically validated. |
| **Diagnostic Performance** | • “Detects 95 % of carriers of cystic fibrosis.” <br>• “Sensitivity of 87 % for hereditary hemochromatosis.” | Performance metrics are reserved for cleared/approved assays. |
| **Medical Efficacy / Clinical Utility** | • “Helps you and your doctor make informed treatment decisions.” <br>• “Reduces the need for invasive procedures by 40 %.” | Suggests the test can influence health outcomes, which is regulated. |
| **Risk Prediction Claims** | • “Predicts your likelihood of developing Parkinson’s disease.” <br>• “Shows a 2‑fold increased risk for heart disease.” | Risk‑prediction language is considered medical claim unless substantiated by cleared data. |
| **Product‑Specific Terminology** | Using brand‑specific names (e.g., “Our **X‑Test**” or “the **New T‑Assay**”) in a way that implies the test already works. | Even naming the product can inadvertently imply functionality. |
| **Comparative Claims** | • “Better than 23andMe for carrier detection.” <br>• “More comprehensive than any other home kit.” | Comparative statements require validated data. |
| **Outcome‑Based Language** | • “Feel confident about your health plan.” <br>• “Take control of your future today.” | Implies the test already provides actionable outcomes. |

**Safe phrasing** – keep language **general**, **educational**, and **future‑focused**:

- “Many people wonder how genetic testing can inform health decisions.”
- “Research shows that understanding variants can be a first step toward personalized healthcare.”
- “When you’re ready to explore your DNA, we’ll be here with a scientifically‑rigorous solution.”

4. **Reference only peer‑reviewed, published literature** when discussing scientific concepts.  
5. **Keep the tone optimistic yet cautious**, emphasizing the promise of genomics while respecting the regulatory timeline.
