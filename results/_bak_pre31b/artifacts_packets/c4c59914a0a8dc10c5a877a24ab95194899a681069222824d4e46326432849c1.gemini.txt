## Anchors

The following anchors define what a 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 look like for this
business unit. Use them as the scoring scale for every answer.

# Finance -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Numbers reconcile and are internally consistent, and the correct concept is applied to the work shape at hand -- accurate AP/AR aging or reconciliation logic for transactional asks, or sound forecast/valuation methodology for strategic asks. Assumptions behind any calculation are stated explicitly, work is shown rather than a bare final number, and the answer is calibrated about when a licensed CPA or controller must sign off.
Example character: a reconciliation or forecast with the formula and inputs shown, an explicit assumption flagged, and a concrete next action (a specific entry, adjustment, or budget line).

## 7 -- Strong
The methodology is correct and the numbers are usable, but one assumption is left unstated, or a reconciliation step is skipped without affecting the bottom line. The work shape (transactional versus strategic) is correctly matched to the ask.
Example character: a correct forecast or reconciliation that does not show its underlying assumptions explicitly.

## 5 -- Adequate
The direction is correct but the numbers are rounded or generic without shown work, or don't fully reconcile against the figures given in the prompt.
Example character: a plausible-looking total that cannot be traced back to the specific inputs provided.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer applies the wrong treatment (conflates cash and accrual basis, misapplies an AP/AR concept), contains a math error, or ignores the specific figures given in the prompt in favor of generic placeholders.
Example character: a calculation that silently mixes cash and accrual figures without flagging the inconsistency.

## 0 -- Unusable
States fabricated financial figures as if sourced or verified, is internally inconsistent to the point of being unusable (totals do not sum, signs are wrong), or gives advice that would misstate a regulated filing with no caveat. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Math that does not reconcile -- totals that do not sum, or an obviously wrong sign.
- Fabricated financial statistics or rates presented as real sourced data.
- Conflating cash and accrual basis without flagging it.
- Advice that would misstate a regulated filing (tax, GAAP) with no caveat that a professional should verify.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Work or formula shown, not just a final number.
- Assumptions behind any calculation stated explicitly.
- Correct AP/AR or forecast-methodology reasoning matched to the stated work shape.
- Explicit flag of when CPA or controller sign-off is needed.

### 3. Trust-to-Fee Transfer Compliance Note

**Do NOT sweep Client A's IOLTA trust balance.**

The $22,000 held in trust is Client A's property under Model Rule of Professional Conduct 1.15. It is legally separate from the firm's operating funds. Applying it to the $18,500 fee invoice without authorization violates ethical rules regarding the safekeeping of client property and could result in bar discipline or civil liability.

**Requirements for a Proper Trust-to-Fee Transfer:**

Before any portion of Client A's $22,000 trust balance can be transferred to the operating account to cover fees, the following must occur:

1.  **Written Client Authorization:** The client must provide a signed **"Client Authorization for Trust-to-Fee Transfer"** form. Verbal consent is insufficient. The form must specify the exact dollar amount to be transferred and the account(s) it is to be applied against.
2.  **Informed Consent:** The client must be explicitly informed that the funds will be removed from the IOLTA ledger and applied to their operating account balance. They must understand that this is a transfer of *their* money.
3.  **Engagement Letter/Retainer Review:** If the original engagement letter does not contain a specific clause authorizing the firm to apply trust advances to operating fees upon written notice, a supplemental addendum must be executed by the client.
4.  **Ledger Adjustment:** Once authorized, the firm's IOLTA bookkeeper must debit the trust account, and the billing manager must credit the operating AR ledger. The audit trail must show the source of the transfer (Client A, Date, Amount, Authorization ID).
5.  **Record Retention:** The signed authorization must be retained in the client's file for the duration of the representation and the statute of limitations for bar complaints.

*Until these conditions are met, the $22,000 trust balance remains off-limits and must be reported separately on any bar association audits or financial disclosures.*

### **PROPER TRUST-TO-FEE TRANSFER REQUIREMENTS**

IOLTA/client trust funds are strictly client property and **cannot be swept** to cover operating fee AR. Before any portion of Client A’s $22,000 trust balance could legally/ethically be applied against their overdue fee invoice, the following must be satisfied per ABA Model Rule 1.15 and typical state bar trust accounting rules:

2. **Written Client Authorization:** The client must provide explicit, signed consent to reallocate the advance from its designated purpose to payment of past due fees.

5. **Proper Ledger Notation:** The disbursement must be recorded as a trust debit → operating account credit, with clear documentation of the client’s authorization, the billing statement, and the earned nature of the funds. No commingling of trust and operating accounts is permitted.
6. **Compliance Review:** The transfer must be reviewed/approved by the firm’s trust accountant or managing partner to ensure strict adherence to state-specific IOLTA regulations and ethical billing standards.

Until all conditions are met, Client A’s $18,500 operating AR must be collected separately through the dunning protocol above. The $22,000 trust balance remains restricted to its authorized purpose (future expert costs) and cannot be applied to fee collections without full compliance.

## 3️⃣  What a **Proper Trust‑to‑Fee Transfer** Looks Like  
(Required before any portion of Client A’s $22,000 IOLTA balance can be applied to the overdue fee.)

| Step | Description | Why it matters |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **1. Written Client Authorization** | Obtain a **signed, dated written request** from Client A authorizing the firm to transfer a specified amount from the IOLTA trust to the operating (fee) account. The request must identify the purpose (e.g., “to cover expert‑witness fees for XYZ litigation”). | ABA Formal Opinion 477 and most state bar rules require an explicit, client‑directed instruction before moving trust funds. |
| **2. Verify the Funds are **Earned** or **Allocated** | Confirm that the amount requested is **directly related to expenses already incurred** (expert‑witness invoices) or that the client has **pre‑approved** the use of trust money for future expert costs. If the transfer is for “future” costs, the client must still sign a separate written agreement stating that the trust funds are being earmarked for that purpose. | Prevents the firm from inadvertently converting client‑owned money into firm revenue before it is earned, which would be a breach of fiduciary duty. |
| **3. Record the Transfer in Both Ledgers** | - **Trust ledger:** Debit the client’s trust balance and credit a “Trust‑to‑Fee Transfer” suspense account. <br> - **Operating ledger:** Debit the “Trust‑to‑Fee Transfer” account and credit Accounts‑Receivable (or directly reduce the overdue invoice). | Provides a clear audit trail; both the trust account and the firm’s general ledger stay in compliance with ABA Model Rule 1.15. |
| **4. Issue a Transfer Confirmation to the Client** | Send a copy of the updated trust ledger and a receipt showing the amount moved and the invoice(s) it now covers. | Demonstrates transparency and satisfies the client’s right to know how their funds were used. |
| **5. Maintain Documentation for 5 Years** | Keep the client’s written request, the transfer journal entries, and any supporting expert‑witness invoices in the client file. | Required for bar‑association audits and for any future disputes. |
| **6. Ensure No Interest or Penalties Apply** | If the transfer occurs after the invoice’s due date, calculate any contractual interest or late fees and apply them to the remaining balance. | Keeps the firm’s fee schedule enforceable and avoids under‑charging. |

**Bottom line:** *Until steps 1‑6 are completed, the $22,000 remains locked in the IOLTA account and cannot be used to offset the $18,500 overdue fee.*
