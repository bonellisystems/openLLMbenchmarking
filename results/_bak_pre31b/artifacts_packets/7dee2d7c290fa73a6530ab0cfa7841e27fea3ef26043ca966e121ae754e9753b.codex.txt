# IT Infrastructure -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The diagnosis or design is correct and tied to the specific systems, symptoms, and constraints given, with exact commands or configuration for the stated platform rather than generic advice. It includes a rollback or backup step before any risky change, quantifies blast radius and expected downtime, and is calibrated about what still needs on-site or live verification. A maintenance-window or change-freeze constraint mentioned in the prompt is respected, not ignored.
Example character: a targeted fix with the exact command sequence, a stated rollback step, and a note on which part of the environment is affected.

## 7 -- Strong
The technical recommendation is sound and directly usable, with correct commands or configuration for the stated platform. It is missing one of: an explicit rollback plan, a quantified blast-radius/downtime estimate, or a post-change verification step. Nothing here would misdiagnose the problem.
Example character: a correct fix for the stated symptom that does not spell out how to undo it if it goes wrong.

## 5 -- Adequate
The direction is correct but the advice reads as a generic troubleshooting checklist rather than being tailored to the specific hardware, software, or symptoms given. An engineer would need to fill in the exact commands and confirm the environment before acting.
Example character: "check logs, restart the service, verify connectivity" offered without adapting to the platform named in the prompt.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer misdiagnoses the stated problem, ignores an explicit constraint (no maintenance window, no downtime allowed, specific hardware), or jumps to a disruptive fix without trying safer steps first.
Example character: recommends a reboot or reinstall as the first step on a production system the prompt flagged as always-on.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends a destructive action (wipe, factory reset, force-delete) with no backup or rollback warning, or invents command syntax or vendor behavior that does not exist. Empty or off-task response.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Destructive commands (rm -rf, drop database, factory reset, force reformat) with no backup or rollback step.
- Fabricated CLI flags, config keys, or vendor documentation presented as real.
- Ignoring a stated production, change-freeze, or maintenance-window constraint.
- No mention of testing a risky change in non-production first.
- Treating a symptom fix as a root-cause fix without saying so.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Explicit rollback or backup step before any risky change.
- Blast radius and expected downtime called out in concrete terms.
- Exact commands or configuration syntax for the stated platform.
- Maintenance-window and change-freeze awareness.
- Post-change verification or monitoring step included.

Dispatchers executed the Continuity of Operations Plan (COOP), reverting to paper/radio procedures. While service was maintained, **911 call-to-dispatch times increased**, posing a risk to public safety response metrics. The vendor attributes the cause to a "database failover that did not complete cleanly during a routine maintenance window."

This postmortem quantifies the service level breach, outlines necessary technical verifications, highlights a critical contractual liability exposure, and recommends immediate remediation steps.

---

### 2. SLA Breach Quantification
The vendor contract mandates **99.9% monthly uptime**. This threshold allows for a maximum of **43.2 minutes** of downtime per 30-day month.

*   **Allowed Downtime (99.9% SLA):** 43.2 minutes per month.
*   **Actual Downtime:** 340 minutes (5 hours 40 minutes).
*   **Breach Magnitude:** The outage consumed **7.87x** the allowable monthly downtime budget.
*   **Result:** This is a **confirmed, material SLA breach**. The vendor is contractually obligated to issue service credits. Additionally, repeated breaches may trigger cure notices or termination rights depending on the "three strikes" clause in the master agreement.

---

### 3. Independent Verification Requirements
The vendor's self-reported root cause ("database failover failure during routine maintenance") is insufficient and potentially masks process failures. Before accepting this explanation, IT and Legal must independently verify the following:

*   **Change Management Authorization:** Confirm via ticketing records whether this "routine maintenance" was submitted for county approval within the required notice period. If maintenance was performed without approval, the root cause is a **contractual violation**, not just a technical failure.
*   **Failover Necessity vs. Error:** Determine if the maintenance window *required* a database failover. If the failover was triggered by a configuration error or script failure during maintenance, the vendor's testing and validation procedures are deficient.
*   **Technical Evidence:** Demand raw logs showing the failover sequence, the specific failure point (e.g., replication lag, split-brain scenario, human intervention), and the exact moment services became unresponsive versus when the failover was initiated.
*   **Testing History:** Review the vendor's records of successful failover tests. If this failover has not been successfully tested in a production-adjacent environment within the last 90 days, the vendor is failing to validate their own DR capabilities.

---

### 4. Critical Business & Legal Risk: Liability Cap Mismatch
**Attention Risk Manager & Legal Counsel:**  
The current contract caps liability for consequential damages at **$50,000**. For a public safety entity, the exposure from a CAD outage—including potential liability for delayed emergency response, reputational harm, regulatory penalties, and recovery costs—vastly exceeds this amount.

*   **Impact:** In the event of a catastrophic failure resulting in harm, the $50k cap is legally insufficient to cover damages.
*   **IT Limitation:** This risk is **structural and contractual**. No IT configuration change, monitoring tool, or technical workaround can mitigate this exposure.
*   **Recommendation:** IT cannot resolve this risk. Legal must immediately evaluate renegotiating the liability cap to reflect the public safety nature of the service, or require the vendor to carry specific cyber/public-safety E&O insurance with adequate limits.

---

### 5. Recommended Resiliency Step
To reduce reliance on manual paper/radio fallback and minimize call-to-dispatch latency during future outages, the county must demand a technical resiliency enhancement:

*   **Action:** **Require the vendor to implement and enable Automated Geo-Redundant Failover with an RTO of < 5 minutes.**
*   **Detail:** The current architecture appears to rely on manual intervention or slow failover mechanisms. The vendor must configure an active-passive or active-active deployment across geographically isolated regions that triggers **automated** failover upon heartbeat loss, eliminating the "cleanliness" of the failover as a variable. This must be validated by a joint tabletop exercise and a live, off-hours failover drill.

---

### 6. Conditions for Accepting Vendor Postmortem
The county should **not** consider this incident closed based on the vendor's current summary. We recommend holding the postmortem as "Preliminary" and demanding the following before final acceptance:

1.  **Evidence-Based Corrective Action Plan (CAP):** A detailed plan with specific engineering tasks, assigned owners, and hard deadlines to prevent recurrence. Vague promises of "improved monitoring" are unacceptable.
2.  **Proof of Remediation:** Evidence that the fix has been applied and, crucially, that the failover mechanism has been **re-tested successfully** in a staging or production environment with results shared with the county.
3.  **SLA Breach Resolution:** Written confirmation of the service credit calculation and application, along with a review of the cumulative SLA history for the contract period.

**Next Steps:**  
The MSP will initiate the formal inquiry to the vendor requesting verification documents and CAP details. Simultaneously, we request a briefing with Legal/Risk to address the liability cap exposure immediately.
