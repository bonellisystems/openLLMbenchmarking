# Panel Judge -- Battery 1 Business Task Scoring

## Role

You are one of three independent judges (Claude, Codex, Gemini) scoring a set
of blinded answers to a single MSP-realistic business task. You do not know
which model produced which answer -- every answer below is labeled only by a
letter. Do not try to guess model identity from writing style. Score only
what is on the page against the anchors below.

## Anchors

The following anchors define what a 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 look like for this
business unit. Use them as the scoring scale for every answer.

# Operations -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The process redesign is tied concretely to the specific bottleneck and constraint stated in the prompt, with measurable KPIs that include a baseline and target, not just a goal restated as a metric. Implementation steps are sequenced with a clear first action, change-management or rollout risk is addressed, and resourcing constraints given in the prompt are respected rather than assumed away.
Example character: a redesign that names the actual bottleneck, gives a baseline-to-target KPI, and sequences the first three implementation steps.

## 7 -- Strong
The process improvement is sound and directly usable, correctly targeting the stated bottleneck. The KPI baseline or rollout sequencing is thinner than ideal, but the core recommendation would work as described.
Example character: a correct fix for the stated bottleneck with a named metric but no baseline value given.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic process-improvement guidance ("streamline the workflow," "reduce handoffs") that is directionally correct but not tied to the specific constraint described in the prompt.
Example character: general efficiency advice that would apply to almost any operational bottleneck.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer misidentifies the bottleneck, or proposes a process that ignores a stated hard constraint (headcount, budget, system limitation), with no measurable KPI anywhere in the response.
Example character: a redesign that requires more staff despite the prompt stating headcount is fixed.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites fabricated industry-benchmark figures as if real, or recommends a change that outright violates a stated hard constraint with no acknowledgment. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Fabricated industry-benchmark numbers presented as real data.
- Ignoring an explicitly stated resource or system constraint.
- No measurable KPI or success criterion anywhere in the response.
- Steps that restate goals rather than being actually sequenced and actionable.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Root cause or bottleneck correctly identified from the details given.
- Measurable KPI with a stated baseline and target.
- Rollout sequenced with a clear, concrete first step.
- Resourcing constraints from the prompt respected in the design.

## Incident Post‑Mortem  
**Title:** Nightly ACH Settlement Batch – Missing Payouts for 60 % of Merchants  
**Date of Incident:** 2024‑10‑31 (production batch)

### 1. Executive Summary  
At 11:15 PM the nightly ACH‑settlement batch, which processes payouts for **4,200 merchants**, failed due to **database‑connection‑pool exhaustion**. The failure halted the batch after it had successfully settled **≈ 40 % (≈ 1,680) of merchants**. Because the automated alert that would have paged the on‑call engineer was filtered out by the engineer’s Do‑Not‑Disturb (DND) settings, **no human was notified until a merchant‑support ticket** arrived at 2:15 AM. Consequently, **≈ 2,520 merchants (60 %) did not receive their scheduled payouts** until the issue was finally resolved ~3 hours later.

The root cause is **not only the database‑pool bug**; the real driver was an **operational process gap** that allowed a critical alert to be silently suppressed and gave no fallback path to detect the failure quickly. Fixing the pool‑exhaustion bug alone would not prevent a repeat if a different failure (e.g., a code regression, external API outage, or configuration error) produced the same “quiet” failure and also bypassed the already‑weak escalation chain.

### 4. Root‑Cause Analysis  

| Layer | Finding | Why it mattered |
|-------|---------|-----------------|
| **Technical (proximate bug)** | **Database connection‑pool exhaustion** caused the batch job to abort after 40 % completion. This was a known, open ticket (low priority) that had not been addressed before the night. | Directly stopped the processing pipeline. |
| **Process / Human** | **Alert suppression** – the critical page was filtered out by the engineer’s DND settings because it was **not on the allow‑list**. The escalation policy relied on a single page that could be silently ignored. | No immediate human acknowledgment; the failure remained invisible for > 3 h. |
| **Monitoring / Observability** | **No secondary health check** (e.g., “batch‑job‑status” metric) that would have triggered an independent alert if the batch remained in “FAILED” state for > 5 min. | Failure could have been detected by an alternate channel (e.g., a watchdog or dashboard). |
| **Operational** | **No fallback escalation** – the policy only escalated after the final retry; there was no “early‑warning” or “fallback” step that would have forced a human to acknowledge the failure sooner. | The 45‑minute window was never exercised because the page never reached the engineer. |
| **Cultural / Process Gap** | **Reliance on manual merchant‑ticket detection** as the only source of incident awareness. | Incident detection was dependent on external customer pain, not on internal safeguards. |

**True driver of the multi‑hour outage:** The **process‑level inability to guarantee that a critical alert reaches an on‑call engineer** and the **absence of an independent detection mechanism**. The database‑pool bug was merely the *trigger*; the *amplifier* was the broken escalation path.

### 6. Why Fixing Only the Database Bug Is Insufficient  

1. **Failure mode independence** – The batch could fail for many reasons (code regression, external API outage, config drift, resource leak, etc.). Each could also be “quiet” and not generate a visible alert.  
2. **Escalation still vulnerable** – Even if the pool‑exhaustion bug were eliminated, a different failure could still cause the batch to stop early and the same DND‑filter‑gap would keep the page from being seen.  
3. **Observability blind spot** – Without an independent health check or a secondary alert channel, any early failure would remain invisible, regardless of the underlying cause.  
4. **Process reliance on a single point of failure** – The on‑call engineer is the only human gatekeeper; if that gatekeeper is unavailable (DND, offline, busy), the entire detection chain collapses.  

Therefore, **the incident would recur** any time a different fault prevented the batch from completing, unless the *process* that ensures the engineer is notified and can act is hardened.

### 4. Proposed Process Fix

**Proposed Fix: Implementation of a "Dead Man’s Switch" for Critical Business Processes.**

Instead of adding headcount or increasing the number of people on call, the team should implement a **time-based "Success Heartbeat" monitor** for all P0 settlement jobs. 

*   **The Logic:** If the ACH settlement job does not report a `SUCCESS` status by a hard deadline (e.g., 12:15 AM), the system triggers a secondary notification to a different communication channel (e.g., a high-priority Slack/Teams channel or a notification to the Support Lead). 
*   **Why this works within constraints:** This does not require new staff. It changes the *trigger* from "the job failed" to "the job has not succeeded by X time." This bypasses the "silent failure" problem: even if the engineer's phone is on DND, the lack of a "Success" signal triggers a business-level alert that ensures visibility through alternative channels (like Support) much faster.

### 4. Proposed Process Fix (No Additional Headcount)  

**Solution: Implement an *Alert‑Redundancy & Verification* layer within the existing on‑call model.**  

1. **Secondary Notification Channel (SMS/Emergency‑Call) for Critical Alerts**  
   - Configure the on‑call system to **automatically send an SMS (or a short voice call) to the on‑call engineer’s mobile** for any alert classified as *Critical* (e.g., nightly batch failures, payment‑processing outages).  
   - SMS bypasses DND on most devices and is less likely to be missed.  

2. **Allow‑list‑Aware DND Handling**  
   - Update the on‑call routing rules so that **any alert tagged with the “Payment‑Batch‑Failure” severity automatically overrides DND** regardless of the engineer’s personal allow‑list.  
   - This can be achieved by assigning a higher *urgency* level that forces the pager‑duty escalation path (page → SMS → phone call) even when DND is active.  

3. **Immediate Ticketing & Auto‑Escalation**  
   - On the first failure detection (after the third automated retry), **auto‑create a high‑priority incident ticket in the ticketing system (e.g., JIRA, ServiceNow)** and **assign it to the on‑call engineer**.  
   - The ticket should have a **visible “Unacknowledged” banner** that appears on the engineer’s dashboard even if they are not looking at the pager console.  

4. **Periodic “Alert Health” Review (15 min sprint)**  
   - Add a **15‑minute “alert‑health check”** at the start of each on‑call shift where the engineer verifies that their notification channels (phone, SMS, email) are receiving test alerts. This is a lightweight, self‑service step that does not add staff.  

**Why this works without extra headcount:**  
- All changes are **configuration / rule updates** in existing tooling (PagerDuty / Opsgenie / ServiceNow).  
- No new personnel are required; the same on‑call engineer continues to own the incident, but now has **multiple, guaranteed delivery mechanisms** and **automatic ticket creation** to surface problems even if one channel fails.

### 5. Why Fixing Only the Database Bug Would Not Prevent Recurrence  

1. **Different Failure Modes:**  
   - The connection‑pool exhaustion is *one* possible failure (resource contention). Future incidents could stem from **network timeouts, third‑party API rate limits, disk I/O saturation, code regression, or schema incompatibility**. Each would trigger a *different* error but would still need rapid human attention.  

2. **Alert‑Delivery Dependency Remains:**  
   - Even if the pool bug is patched, any *new* critical failure will still rely on the same alert‑delivery pipeline. If the engineer’s phone remains on DND or the alert is filtered out, the incident will again sit silently until a downstream symptom (e.g., a merchant support ticket) surfaces it—reproducing the multi‑hour delay.  

3. **Process‑Level Risk vs. Technical Risk:**  
   - The **process risk** (notification failure) is *independent* of the **technical risk** (specific bug). Eliminating the technical risk does **not** eliminate the process risk. Without a robust, redundant alerting path, any critical failure—no matter how trivial to fix—can become a prolonged outage.  

Therefore, **addressing only the database connection‑pool bug would leave the organization vulnerable to the same class of “silent‑outage” incidents caused by alert‑delivery failures**, leading again to long customer‑impact windows.
