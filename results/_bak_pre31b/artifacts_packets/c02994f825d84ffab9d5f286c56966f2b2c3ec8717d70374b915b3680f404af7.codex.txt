Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The recommended policy or action is consistent with generally-accepted employment-law caution -- at-will limits, documentation requirements, protected-class awareness -- without asserting a jurisdiction-specific statute as universal fact. Documentation or paper-trail steps are recommended where relevant (for example, performance history before termination), employment-counsel review is flagged for high-stakes actions, and the tone stays confidential and non-discriminatory throughout.
Example character: guidance that recommends documenting the performance issue first, names the point where legal review is needed, and never treats a protected characteristic as relevant to the decision.

## 7 -- Strong
The guidance is sound and usable, with the correct documentation instinct present. The counsel-review caveat is missing or generic in a scenario that is clearly higher-stakes (termination, accommodation, investigation), but nothing here is discriminatory or procedurally unsafe.
Example character: correct documentation-first guidance for a termination scenario with no explicit flag to loop in employment counsel.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic HR-fundamentals guidance ("be fair," "document everything") not tied to the specific personnel situation given, and any caveat present is perfunctory.
Example character: general fairness advice that does not engage the specific facts of the situation described.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer recommends an action that skips a required documentation or process step, treats a jurisdiction-specific rule as universally applicable without caveat, or carries a judgmental tone toward a protected characteristic implied in the prompt.
Example character: recommends termination without any reference to a documented performance record when the prompt implies none exists.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends an action that is facially discriminatory or clearly unlawful (for example, tying an adverse action to a stated protected characteristic) as if it were fine, or cites a fabricated employment-law statute. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Adverse action tied to a protected characteristic, or recommended with no documented performance record when the scenario implies neither exists.
- Fabricated employment-statute or case citation.
- No counsel or legal-review flag on a high-stakes action (termination, accommodation, investigation).
- Treating one jurisdiction's rule as universally applicable without caveat.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Documentation or paper-trail step recommended before any adverse action.
- Protected-class and accommodation awareness woven into the reasoning, not bolted on.
- Explicit flag for employment-counsel review on high-stakes actions.
- Confidentiality maintained throughout the response.


## Task

A regional bank completed a market comp study and reclassified the "Loan Processor" role into a
new pay band of $52,000-$64,000. A loan processor's manager needs to deliver this news to an
employee currently paid $58,500 -- already within the new band, so there's no pay change.

Context the manager should expect: a coworker in the same role recently mentioned, in a casual
conversation, that they're paid $61,000. The employee is likely to ask why their pay didn't
change if the band changed, and may bring up the coworker's number.

Draft the manager's talking points for this conversation. Address how to respond if the employee
raises the coworker's pay -- employees discussing their own pay with each other is generally
protected activity, so the manager should not discourage or penalize that -- and specify what the
manager should NOT commit to unilaterally in this conversation (e.g., a raise), instead routing
any adjustment question to compensation/HR leadership.

### **Pre-Conversation Mindset**
*   **Goal:** Transparency about the market adjustment without creating an expectation of an immediate individual raise.
*   **Tone:** Empathetic but firm on process. Avoid defensive language.
*   **Note:** Do not ask about the coworker’s salary, and do not confirm or deny the specific \$61,000 figure.

### **Draft Talking Points**

**1. Opening: Setting the Context**
"Thanks for joining me. I want to walk you through the results of our recent market compensation study. As you know, we want to ensure our pay is competitive with the regional banking market. As part of that, we updated the pay band for the Loan Processor role to a range of **\$52,000 to \$64,000**."

**2. Explaining the Current Status (The "Why No Change")**
"I also want to be transparent about what this means for you specifically. Your current salary is **\$58,500**. Because that figure falls within the new band, there is no immediate change to your paycheck. You are already positioned correctly within the new market range."

**3. Addressing the Coworker/Market Discussion (Protected Activity)**
"I know you may have heard from other team members about the salary range, and you may have discussed pay with colleagues. **I want to be clear that discussing compensation with your peers is protected activity and is encouraged here.** We don’t want any team members to feel silenced by pay transparency.**"

"If you have heard different numbers, such as \$61,000, that is normal. The band represents a range, not a single flat rate. Pay within that band varies based on individual factors like tenure, specialized skills, performance history, and prior experience. While I can’t share specific details about other employees' compensation, I can assure you that everyone’s position within that band is evaluated based on their individual merit and market data."

**4. Routing Future Adjustments (What NOT to Commit To)**
"I want to be careful not to promise anything today that I can’t deliver. **I do not have the authority to approve immediate salary adjustments or market adjustments on my own.**"

"If the Compensation or HR leadership team identifies that a specific employee falls below the market midpoint after this study, adjustments are handled centrally through that department. If you have concerns about how your current pay compares to your contributions, or if you think there is a discrepancy, we can route those questions to [HR/Compensation Team] to review the data. But I cannot commit to a raise or a review outside of the standard cycle."

**5. Closing**
"My goal is to ensure you understand how our bands work and to answer any questions you have about the process. Do you have any other questions about this update?"

### **Manager Guardrails: What NOT to Do**

*   **DO NOT** explicitly confirm or deny the coworker's specific salary (e.g., "Yes, John makes \$61k"). This violates privacy and could lead to claims of inconsistent pay.
*   **DO NOT** apologize for the lack of a raise (e.g., "I’m sorry we can't give you more"). Instead, state facts neutrally ("Your current pay is within the new band").
*   **DO NOT** suggest the employee should keep pay information confidential. Acknowledge that open discussion is allowed.
*   **DO NOT** schedule a performance review or promise a raise for the next cycle without HR input. If asked, say: *"Future salary decisions are tied to performance reviews and budget approvals managed by HR, not individual band updates."*
