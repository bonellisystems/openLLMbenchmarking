## 2. Client‑Non‑Payment & Immediate Termination of the Engagement

### What I can tell you (general information)

- **Statutory/Rule‑based withdrawal:** Many jurisdictions permit a lawyer to withdraw from a matter **if the client fails to pay** and the lawyer provides **reasonable notice** (often 30 days) of the intention to withdraw, unless the withdrawal would cause “substantial prejudice” to the client.  
- **Engagement‑letter language:** Even when the letter is silent on withdrawal, the default rule is that you may still terminate **provided you give the client reasonable notice** and return any papers or property belonging to the client.  
- **Potential exposure:** Terminating **without notice**—especially when the client may still be liable for fees—can be viewed as a breach of professional conduct rules and may open the door to a claim for “wrongful discharge” or for the client to seek a court order for continuation of representation.

### Why this is a matter that requires **licensed counsel** (and why I can’t give you definitive advice)

1. **Jurisdiction‑specific rules** – The exact notice period, permissible methods of termination, and any required filing with a court or bar authority differ by state/province and sometimes by practice area.  
2. **Ethical obligations** – The ABA Model Rules (and most state equivalents) require that a lawyer “withdraw from a matter” only after **taking reasonable steps** to avoid prejudicing the client’s rights. Failure to follow those steps can itself be a disciplinary violation.  
3. **Potential contractual nuance** – While the engagement letter may be silent, other documents (e.g., a retainer agreement, a standing fee schedule, or prior communications) might create implied terms that affect your right to terminate.  
4. **Risk of litigation** – If the client alleges they were “abandoned” without notice, they could bring a claim for damages, which would need to be evaluated in the context of the specific facts, the jurisdiction’s statutes of limitation, and any applicable privilege or confidentiality considerations.

Because of those factors, **I cannot advise you on the precise wording of a termination notice, the legal sufficiency of an “immediate” termination, or the risk of a lawsuit**—those are matters that only a licensed attorney in the firm can evaluate after reviewing the complete file, the applicable rules, and any relevant case law.

### How we should proceed

- **Schedule a brief call** with the supervising attorney (or the firm’s risk‑management/compliance officer) to discuss the client’s payment status and the appropriate withdrawal procedure.  
- **Draft a formal notice** (letter or email) that meets the jurisdiction’s notice requirements and includes:  
  1. A clear statement of termination (effective on a specific date),  
  2. The reason (non‑payment),  
  3. A request for any outstanding fees, and  
  4. Instructions for the return of client property/records.  
- **Maintain a written record** of all communications regarding the termination, including the date you sent the notice, the content of the notice, and the client’s response (or lack thereof). This documentation is vital if a dispute later arises.
