# Helpdesk -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Triage priority matches the actual business impact described, and the response tone is consistent with the urgency that priority implies. Troubleshooting steps are ordered from least to most disruptive, a clear escalation path is given for when the fix is out of the agent's scope, and the answer is calibrated about what genuinely needs remote hands or an on-site visit.
Example character: a triage call that matches the stated impact, a non-disruptive-first troubleshooting sequence, and a named escalation trigger.

## 7 -- Strong
The troubleshooting sequence is usable and the priority call is correct, but the escalation path or SLA framing is a light miss -- present but not fully specified. Nothing here jumps to a disruptive step too early.
Example character: a correct non-disruptive troubleshooting sequence with no stated escalation trigger if it fails.

## 5 -- Adequate
The response is a generic troubleshooting checklist (restart, check cables, reinstall) not tied to the specific symptom or business impact described in the ticket.
Example character: standard first-line steps offered without adapting to the reported symptom.

## 3 -- Weak
The priority or severity call is wrong relative to the stated impact, or the response skips straight to a disruptive fix (reimage, factory reset) without trying non-disruptive steps first, with no escalation path given.
Example character: recommends reimaging a device as the first step for a minor, non-blocking symptom.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends a data-destructive action (wipe, reformat, delete profile) with no backup step or warning, or fabricates vendor or product behavior presented as known fact. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Destructive troubleshooting step (reimage, factory reset, delete profile) recommended before non-disruptive steps, or with no backup warning.
- Priority/SLA call that does not match the stated business impact.
- No escalation path given when the issue is clearly out of scope.
- Fabricated product or vendor behavior presented as known fact.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Triage priority matches the stated business impact.
- Troubleshooting steps ordered least-to-most disruptive.
- Explicit escalation trigger and path given.
- SLA/urgency framing consistent with the ticket's actual severity.


## Task

The task given to every model in this cohort:

An architecture firm's senior architect reports that the shared Revit central model (the
master worksharing file that the project team links their local files to) won't open --
Revit reports the central model file may be corrupted. Cause: last night, the helpdesk
performed a scheduled after-hours reboot of the file server at 11:40 PM without first
checking for active Revit worksharing sessions. Revit's sync logs show 3 team members still
had local sessions open with unsynced local changes at that moment. There's a client
presentation with a design-development deadline in 26 hours. The file server's last
successful nightly backup completed at 10:00 PM -- 1 hour 40 minutes before the reboot -- so
restoring from that backup would lose only the 3 architects' changes made in that 1h40m
window, nothing earlier. Nobody on the helpdesk team is trained in Revit central-model
recovery internals, but Autodesk and independent BIM-specialist consultants offer emergency
support for exactly this kind of corruption. Decide and justify, in order: what to check
before touching the corrupted central model at all, whether to restore from the 10:00 PM
backup immediately (and what that costs each of the 3 architects), and when/whether to
escalate to Autodesk or a BIM specialist rather than have helpdesk attempt the repair itself.

Here is a structured, step-by-step decision framework aligned with BIM best practices and the constraints of your scenario.

### 1. What to check before touching the corrupted central model at all
**Decisions/Checks:**
- **Freeze access:** Immediately enforce a strict `No Open / No Repair` policy on the corrupted file. Any attempt to open, recover, or overwrite it can permanently damage Revit’s embedded worksharing database.
- **Verify the exact error & Revit version:** Determine if the failure is a worksharing database corruption, a transaction log mismatch, or actual geometry/file structure damage. Error codes dictate recovery path.
- **Scan the central model folder for Revit auto-backups:** Revit automatically creates timestamped `.rvt` backup files in the same directory during successful syncs. Identify the most recent intact backup (often newer than the 10:00 PM server backup).
- **Locate & isolate the 3 architects’ local files:** Ensure the local `.rvt` files on each workstation are copied to a secure, offline location. These contain the unsynced changes and are your primary recovery asset.
- **Validate server health & permissions:** Run quick I/O, disk health, and NTFS/SharePoint permission checks. Intermittent network drops or ACL changes during reboot can mimic or exacerbate corruption.
- **Confirm worksharing state at 10:00 PM:** Check sync logs or BIM 360/Project Folder audit trails to verify that the central model was in a clean, fully synced state at backup time.

**Justification:**  
Revit’s worksharing architecture embeds a relational database inside the central file. An abrupt server reboot during active sessions often leaves transaction commits half-written. Touching the file without preserving locals, auto-backups, or diagnosing the failure mode risks overwriting recoverable data or compounding database inconsistencies. These checks establish a safe recovery baseline, identify the best available recovery point, and prevent irreversible damage before any action is taken.

### 2. Whether to restore from the 10:00 PM backup immediately (and cost to the 3 architects)
**Decision:**  
**Yes, restore immediately**, but restore to a *new filename/location* first, validate the worksharing state, and only promote it to central once confirmed stable.

**Justification:**  
With a 26-hour design-development deadline, minimizing downtime is the highest priority. The 10:00 PM backup represents a known-good, fully synced central state. Restoring it eliminates corruption risk, resets the worksharing database cleanly, and allows work to resume predictably. The alternative (waiting for expert repair or attempting DIY fixes) introduces unacceptable schedule risk.

**Cost to each of the 3 architects:**
- **Time/effort:** ~2–4 hours per architect to extract their local file, review pending changes, and re-sync them into the restored central model.
- **Coordination overhead:** Potential need to resolve minor version conflicts if their changes overlap with edits synced by others before 10:00 PM.

**Bottom line:** The cost is low, predictable, and fully justified by the guarantee of a working central model within hours. In BIM project management, losing 1–2 hours of recent work to secure a 26-hour deadline is standard risk mitigation.
