# Panel Judge -- Battery 1 Business Task Scoring

## Role

You are one of three independent judges (Claude, Codex, Gemini) scoring a set
of blinded answers to a single MSP-realistic business task. You do not know
which model produced which answer -- every answer below is labeled only by a
letter. Do not try to guess model identity from writing style. Score only
what is on the page against the anchors below.

## Anchors

The following anchors define what a 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 look like for this
business unit. Use them as the scoring scale for every answer.

# HR & People Ops -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
The recommended policy or action is consistent with generally-accepted employment-law caution -- at-will limits, documentation requirements, protected-class awareness -- without asserting a jurisdiction-specific statute as universal fact. Documentation or paper-trail steps are recommended where relevant (for example, performance history before termination), employment-counsel review is flagged for high-stakes actions, and the tone stays confidential and non-discriminatory throughout.
Example character: guidance that recommends documenting the performance issue first, names the point where legal review is needed, and never treats a protected characteristic as relevant to the decision.

## 7 -- Strong
The guidance is sound and usable, with the correct documentation instinct present. The counsel-review caveat is missing or generic in a scenario that is clearly higher-stakes (termination, accommodation, investigation), but nothing here is discriminatory or procedurally unsafe.
Example character: correct documentation-first guidance for a termination scenario with no explicit flag to loop in employment counsel.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic HR-fundamentals guidance ("be fair," "document everything") not tied to the specific personnel situation given, and any caveat present is perfunctory.
Example character: general fairness advice that does not engage the specific facts of the situation described.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer recommends an action that skips a required documentation or process step, treats a jurisdiction-specific rule as universally applicable without caveat, or carries a judgmental tone toward a protected characteristic implied in the prompt.
Example character: recommends termination without any reference to a documented performance record when the prompt implies none exists.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends an action that is facially discriminatory or clearly unlawful (for example, tying an adverse action to a stated protected characteristic) as if it were fine, or cites a fabricated employment-law statute. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Adverse action tied to a protected characteristic, or recommended with no documented performance record when the scenario implies neither exists.
- Fabricated employment-statute or case citation.
- No counsel or legal-review flag on a high-stakes action (termination, accommodation, investigation).
- Treating one jurisdiction's rule as universally applicable without caveat.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Documentation or paper-trail step recommended before any adverse action.
- Protected-class and accommodation awareness woven into the reasoning, not bolted on.
- Explicit flag for employment-counsel review on high-stakes actions.
- Confidentiality maintained throughout the response.


## Task

The task given to every model in this cohort:

A small retail-services business just hired its first-ever operations coordinator, starting in
2 weeks. The hiring manager wants a plan covering:

1. Pre-boarding paperwork, including federal employment-eligibility verification (Form I-9),

3. A first-week schedule and a buddy assignment.

Build the onboarding plan covering these four points. Note briefly whether anything about this
role's pay classification (exempt vs. non-exempt) should be double-checked against actual job
duties before finalizing the pay-related paperwork, rather than assumed from the job title.

## 5. Pay Classification – Quick‑Check Reminder

> **Before finalizing any payroll documentation, verify that the Operations Coordinator’s exemption status matches the actual duties performed.**  
> - **Non‑exempt (hourly) vs. Exempt (salary)** depends on factors such as:  
>   - Primary job duties (administrative vs. managerial vs. operational)  
>   - Frequency of supervising others  
>   - Degree of discretion over day‑to‑day decisions  
>   - Salary level test (if salaried)  
> - **Action:**  
>   1. Conduct a **job‑description analysis** (using the Department of Labor’s “white‑collar” criteria).  
>   2. Compare the actual responsibilities (e.g., scheduling staff, vendor negotiations, budget monitoring) against the criteria for exemption.  
>   3. Document the determination and retain it with the employee’s personnel file.  
>   4. Adjust the offer letter and payroll classification accordingly **prior to the first pay date**.  

> *This step should be completed by HR/Compensation in parallel with the pre‑boarding paperwork to avoid retroactive pay‑adjustments.*

### **5. Pay Classification Note: Exempt vs. Non-Exempt**
*Please double-check the pay classification against actual duties, not just the title.*

*   **Warning:** The title "Operations Coordinator" can imply both exempt and non-exempt roles. Misclassification carries significant legal and financial risk, especially in a small retail environment.
*   **Key Factors to Verify:**
    *   **Nature of Duties:** Does the role require professional, administrative, or management tasks (e.g., managing schedules, analyzing data, making decisions) as the *primary* duty? Or is it largely execution-based, hourly monitoring, or present in the store during shifts?
    *   **Work Environment:** In retail, if the coordinator is expected to work irregular hours, be on the floor during peaks, or is required to work overtime without pay, this strongly suggests **Non-Exempt** status.
    *   **Time Tracking:** If the role is Non-Exempt, ensure time-tracking systems are active immediately. If Exempt, ensure the salary is sufficient and duties meet the "white-collar" test.
    *   **Action:** Review the job description against Department of Labor (DOL) tests. If there is any ambiguity, consult with a labor attorney or HR professional to confirm classification before finalizing the pay structure and W-2 forms.

## ⚠️ Pay Classification Note: Exempt vs. Non-Exempt
**Do not assume exempt status based on the title "Operations Coordinator."**

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), exemption from overtime pay depends on **both** a salary level test **and** a duties test. An Operations Coordinator title often implies administrative duties, which may qualify for the "white-collar" exemption, but only if:
1.  **Salary Level:** The employee is paid on a salary basis at or above the minimum threshold ($684/week federal minimum, though many states require higher).
2.  **Duties Test:** The primary duty is office or non-manual work, exercising discretion and independent judgment on significant matters.

**Action:** Before finalizing the pay-related paperwork (offer letter, timesheet setup), the Hiring Manager should review the actual written job description. If the role involves significant manual labor, direct client service, or hourly tasks, it must be classified as **non-exempt**, and the business must implement an accurate timesheet and overtime calculation system. Misclassification carries significant legal risk, especially for a first-time employer with no prior payroll infrastructure.

## 5. Pay‑Classification Check – Exempt vs. Non‑Exempt  

**Why it matters:**  
- The Operations Coordinator title *sounds* like a **non‑exempt (hourly)** role, but the actual duties (e.g., discretion over scheduling, independent decision‑making, supervising other staff) may qualify it as **exempt (salaried)** under the **FLSA “administrative”** or “executive” exemption.  
- Mis‑classifying an employee can lead to wage‑and‑hour penalties, overtime disputes, and payroll errors.

**Action steps before finalizing payroll paperwork:**  

1. **Review the finalized job description** against the three FLSA exemption tests:  
   - *Salary basis*: ≥ $684 per week (2024 threshold).  
   - *Primary duties*: (a) Managing the day‑to‑day operations of a department, (b) exercising discretion and independent judgment on matters of significance, (c) supervising or influencing the work of other employees.  
2. **Confirm with legal/HR** that the duties listed (e.g., “authorizes overtime for staff, approves vendor contracts, creates operational policies”) meet the *administrative* or *executive* exemption criteria.  
3. **If any doubt remains**, default to **non‑exempt** (hourly) for the first payroll cycle and re‑classify after a formal review—this avoids accidental overtime violations.  
4. **Document the decision** (email trail or HR file) and reflect it on the **I‑9/Payroll set‑up forms** (W‑4, state tax, direct deposit).  

*Bottom line:* **Double‑check the exemption status now**—don’t assume the “Coordinator” title automatically means non‑exempt. A brief meeting between the Hiring Manager, HR, and the company’s legal counsel (15 minutes) should settle the classification before the employee’s first paycheck is processed.
