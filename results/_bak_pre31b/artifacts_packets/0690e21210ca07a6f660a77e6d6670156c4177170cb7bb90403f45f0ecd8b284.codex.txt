## Why Blending Would Be Misleading

Blending all three cohorts into a single "average retention" number would be misleading because:

1. **Different time horizons:** The cohorts represent different age groups (3 months old, 2 months old, and 1 month old respectively)

2. **Incomplete data:** The younger cohorts don't have full retention history, making direct comparisons invalid

3. **Different business contexts:** Each cohort represents different market conditions and customer behaviors

4. **Time-based trends:** The retention behavior may be changing over time, so combining cohorts at different stages would obscure the true retention patterns

The proper approach is to analyze each cohort's retention at its appropriate time point (Month 2 for Jan and Feb cohorts, Month 3 for Jan cohort only).

*   **Jan Cohort:** Is 3 months old (75% retention).

**1.  Net‑Dollar Retention (NDR) – what it means**  
NDR for a given month = (MRR retained in that month ÷ MRR that existed in the signup month) × 100 %.  
When we talk about “Month‑2 NDR” we compare the **second‑month** revenue to the **first‑month** (signup) revenue; “Month‑3 NDR” does the same for the third month.

## 4.  Why blending all three cohorts into a single “average retention” right now would be misleading

1. **Different ages (different stages of the retention curve)**  
   - Jan cohort: 3 months old → we can compute month‑1, month‑2, **and month‑3** retention.  
   - Feb cohort: 2 months old → we can compute month‑1 and month‑2, but **no month‑3**.  
   - Mar cohort: 1 month old → we only have month‑1 data.  

   Each cohort is at a distinct point on its churn/retention trajectory. Averaging the three retention percentages treats a brand‑new cohort the same as an already‑mature one, which distorts the true picture of how retention behaves over time.

2. **Sample‑size weighting**  
   - 40 customers in Jan, 50 in Feb, 35 in Mar.  
   - A naïve average would give each cohort an equal *percentage* weight, even though the Jan cohort represents fewer customers than the Feb cohort. The resulting “average” would be dominated by the smaller, newer cohorts rather than reflecting the overall business performance.

3. **Retention dynamics are time‑dependent**  
   - Early‑stage churn (month 2) is often larger than later‑stage churn (month 3, month 4, …).  
   - If we blindly combine them, the early‑stage churn of the Mar cohort (which we cannot even measure yet) would artificially pull the average up or down depending on the arbitrary weighting method.

4. **Business‑decision relevance**  
   - Leadership typically wants to know “what percent of the MRR we signed up **two months ago** are we still keeping **right now**?”  
   - That question can only be answered by looking at the **Feb‑2026 cohort** (which is exactly two months past its signup).  
   - Mixing in the Jan cohort (now three months old) and the Mar cohort (still one month old) would answer a different question altogether.

**Bottom line:**  
Only **cohorts of the same vintage** (same number of months since signup) can be meaningfully aggregated for a comparable retention metric. At this snapshot, the Jan, Feb, and Mar cohorts are at month‑3, month‑2, and month‑1 respectively, so any blended “average retention” would be a statistical artifact, not a genuine measure of any real‑world retention stage.
