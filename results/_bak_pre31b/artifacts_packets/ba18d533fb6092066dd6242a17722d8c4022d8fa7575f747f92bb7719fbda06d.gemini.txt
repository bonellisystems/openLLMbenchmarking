# Operations -- scoring anchors (0-10)

## 10 -- Frontier
The process redesign is tied concretely to the specific bottleneck and constraint stated in the prompt, with measurable KPIs that include a baseline and target, not just a goal restated as a metric. Implementation steps are sequenced with a clear first action, change-management or rollout risk is addressed, and resourcing constraints given in the prompt are respected rather than assumed away.
Example character: a redesign that names the actual bottleneck, gives a baseline-to-target KPI, and sequences the first three implementation steps.

## 7 -- Strong
The process improvement is sound and directly usable, correctly targeting the stated bottleneck. The KPI baseline or rollout sequencing is thinner than ideal, but the core recommendation would work as described.
Example character: a correct fix for the stated bottleneck with a named metric but no baseline value given.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic process-improvement guidance ("streamline the workflow," "reduce handoffs") that is directionally correct but not tied to the specific constraint described in the prompt.
Example character: general efficiency advice that would apply to almost any operational bottleneck.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer misidentifies the bottleneck, or proposes a process that ignores a stated hard constraint (headcount, budget, system limitation), with no measurable KPI anywhere in the response.
Example character: a redesign that requires more staff despite the prompt stating headcount is fixed.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites fabricated industry-benchmark figures as if real, or recommends a change that outright violates a stated hard constraint with no acknowledgment. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Fabricated industry-benchmark numbers presented as real data.
- Ignoring an explicitly stated resource or system constraint.
- No measurable KPI or success criterion anywhere in the response.
- Steps that restate goals rather than being actually sequenced and actionable.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Root cause or bottleneck correctly identified from the details given.
- Measurable KPI with a stated baseline and target.
- Rollout sequenced with a clear, concrete first step.
- Resourcing constraints from the prompt respected in the design.

### 1. Station Effective Throughput Rates

First, we must normalize the **Chill Tumbler** from a batch process to an average continuous rate to compare it fairly with the other stations.

### 2. The True Line-Limiting Bottleneck

**The True Bottleneck is Station 3: Portioning/Cut-Up (38 units/min).**

In a series line, the bottleneck is the station with the **lowest effective throughput rate**, not the station with the most visible inventory.

| Station | Process Type | Effective Rate (units/min) | Daily Capacity (8 hrs) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. Evisceration | Continuous | 50 | 24,000 |
| 2. Chill Tumbler | Batch (Avg) | 50 | 24,000 |
| **3. Portioning** | **Continuous** | **38** | **18,240** |
| 4. Packaging | Continuous | 42 | 20,160 |

**Why it is NOT the Chill Station (despite the queue):**
The floor supervisors are confusing **Work-In-Process (WIP) inventory** with **throughput capacity**.
1.  **Batch Intermittency:** The Chill Tumbler processes units in lumps. It accumulates 900 units (taking $900 / 50 = 18$ minutes of continuous feed) before processing them over 20 minutes. This creates a massive, concentrated pile of carcasses at the tumbler entrance. This pile is "visible" and looks like a backlog.
2.  **Throughput Equivalence:** Once averaged, the Chill Tumbler outputs 50 units/min—the same speed Evisceration feeds it. There is no accumulation over time at the Chill station itself (input = output = 50). The pile is a mechanical artifact of batching, not a capacity constraint.
3.  **The Real Constraint:** Portioning runs at **38 units/min**. Because the input to Portioning (50 units/min from Chill/Evisceration) is significantly higher than its output (38 units/min), every unit that passes through Portioning is immediately blocked. This causes a permanent, growing queue at Portioning that limits the *entire line's daily flow*.

### 3. Daily Output & Shortfall

*   **Shift Duration:** 8 hours = 480 minutes.
*   **Line Output:** Determined by the bottleneck (Portioning).
*   **Maximum Achievable Daily Output:**
    $$38 \text{ units/min} \times 480 \text{ min} = \mathbf{18,240 \text{ units/day}}$$
*   **Daily Target:** 20,000 units.
*   **Shortfall:**
    $$20,000 - 18,240 = \mathbf{1,760 \text{ units/day}}$$

The plant is producing roughly 8.8% below target every single day.

### 4. Minimum Rate Required for Target

To hit the 20,000-unit target in 480 minutes, Portioning must sustain the following rate:

$$\text{Required Rate} = \frac{20,000 \text{ units}}{480 \text{ min}} \approx \mathbf{41.67 \text{ units/min}}$$

Portioning must increase from **38 units/min to at least 42 units/min** (rounding up to the nearest whole unit rate). This requires a **10.5% improvement** in the current portioning speed.

### 5. Warning: Do NOT Add a Second Chill Tumbler

**This fix will not increase daily output at all.**

Adding a second Chill Tumbler effectively doubles the batch processing capacity.
*   **New Chill Capacity:** $2 \times (1,000 / 20) = 100 \text{ units/min}$ (avg).
*   **New Line Bottleneck:** Portioning remains at **38 units/min** (since $100 > 38$).

**Why it fails:**
1.  **Upstream of the Constraint:** The Chill Tumbler is upstream of the true bottleneck (Portioning). In a series line, adding capacity to a station that is *not* the bottleneck has zero effect on total line throughput. The line cannot push water out of a hose faster than the nozzle allows, regardless of how much water is pumped into the hose.
2.  **WIP Shift:** The queue of units will simply disappear from the Chill station and re-appear at the Portioning station. The "visible queue" problem moves downstream rather than being solved.
3.  **Financial Waste:** You will spend capital on a second tumbler, extra floor space, and maintenance staff, gaining exactly **0 additional units per day**.

**Correct Fix:** Invest in increasing the rate of Station 3 (Portioning/Cut-up) to 42 units/min. This moves the bottleneck past the current constraint and allows the line to reach the 20,000-unit target.

*\*Chill tumbler rated capacity: 1,000 units / 20 min cycle = 50 units/min. The 900-unit trigger is a minimum run condition, not a capacity reducer.*

**Chill Tumbler Batch Math (Steady-State Dynamics):**
- Upstream feeds at 50 units/min. Time to hit the 900-unit trigger: `900 ÷ 50 = 18 min`.
- Tumbler runs for 20 min, outputting up to 1,000 units.
- During that 20-min run, upstream continues feeding: `50 × 20 = 1,000 units` enter the queue.
- Queue evolution: Starts at 900 → grows to 1,900 during cycle → tumbler removes 1,000 → ends at 900.
- **Result:** The queue never drops below 900 units. The tumbler runs back-to-back every 20 min in steady state, processing exactly 1,000 units per cycle.
- **Effective throughput:** `1,000 units ÷ 20 min = 50 units/min` (3,000/hr, 24,000/shift).

### 2. The TRUE Line-Limiting Bottleneck

**Bottleneck: Station 3 – Portioning/Cut-up (38 units/min)**

Theory of Constraints defines the bottleneck as **the resource with the lowest effective capacity that determines total system output**, not where inventory visibly accumulates. Comparing effective rates:
- Evisceration: 50/min
- Chill: 50/min
- **Portioning: 38/min** ← Lowest
- Packaging: 42/min

Portioning can only pull 38 units/min, regardless of how much product sits ahead of it. It physically caps the entire line.

**Why Chill *Looks* Like the Bottleneck (But Isn't):**
Floor supervisors see a large standing queue at the chill in-feed and correctly identify it as high-WIP, but **WIP ≠ throughput constraint**. The 900-unit accumulation trigger is a deliberate design feature for chill efficacy, not a failure of capacity. The queue exists because:
1. The tumbler cannot accept units continuously; it requires a minimum batch.
2. The 20-min cycle creates a predictable "fill-drain" pattern, leaving ~900 units perpetually buffered.
3. This visible pile is **scheduled inventory**, not stuck inventory. It flows through at 50 units/min in steady state, which exceeds downstream demand. The queue is a symptom of batch scheduling, not a throughput ceiling.

This is the classic "parallel capacity fallacy." Adding a second tumbler addresses **queue size**, not **system throughput**. Numbers prove it:

- What *does* change: The standing queue at chill drops from ~900 units to ~450 units, improving floor space and reducing wait-time variability. But **daily shipped units stay at 18,240**.
