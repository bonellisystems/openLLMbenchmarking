## 10 -- Frontier
The recommended policy or action is consistent with generally-accepted employment-law caution -- at-will limits, documentation requirements, protected-class awareness -- without asserting a jurisdiction-specific statute as universal fact. Documentation or paper-trail steps are recommended where relevant (for example, performance history before termination), employment-counsel review is flagged for high-stakes actions, and the tone stays confidential and non-discriminatory throughout.
Example character: guidance that recommends documenting the performance issue first, names the point where legal review is needed, and never treats a protected characteristic as relevant to the decision.

## 7 -- Strong
The guidance is sound and usable, with the correct documentation instinct present. The counsel-review caveat is missing or generic in a scenario that is clearly higher-stakes (termination, accommodation, investigation), but nothing here is discriminatory or procedurally unsafe.
Example character: correct documentation-first guidance for a termination scenario with no explicit flag to loop in employment counsel.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is generic HR-fundamentals guidance ("be fair," "document everything") not tied to the specific personnel situation given, and any caveat present is perfunctory.
Example character: general fairness advice that does not engage the specific facts of the situation described.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer recommends an action that skips a required documentation or process step, treats a jurisdiction-specific rule as universally applicable without caveat, or carries a judgmental tone toward a protected characteristic implied in the prompt.
Example character: recommends termination without any reference to a documented performance record when the prompt implies none exists.

## 0 -- Unusable
Recommends an action that is facially discriminatory or clearly unlawful (for example, tying an adverse action to a stated protected characteristic) as if it were fine, or cites a fabricated employment-law statute. Empty or off-task.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Adverse action tied to a protected characteristic, or recommended with no documented performance record when the scenario implies neither exists.
- Fabricated employment-statute or case citation.
- No counsel or legal-review flag on a high-stakes action (termination, accommodation, investigation).
- Treating one jurisdiction's rule as universally applicable without caveat.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Documentation or paper-trail step recommended before any adverse action.
- Protected-class and accommodation awareness woven into the reasoning, not bolted on.
- Explicit flag for employment-counsel review on high-stakes actions.
- Confidentiality maintained throughout the response.


## Task

Context the manager should expect: a coworker in the same role recently mentioned, in a casual
conversation, that they're paid $61,000. The employee is likely to ask why their pay didn't
change if the band changed, and may bring up the coworker's number.

Draft the manager's talking points for this conversation. Address how to respond if the employee
raises the coworker's pay -- employees discussing their own pay with each other is generally
protected activity, so the manager should not discourage or penalize that -- and specify what the
manager should NOT commit to unilaterally in this conversation (e.g., a raise), instead routing
any adjustment question to compensation/HR leadership.

Here are the manager talking points and guidelines for this conversation.

### **Conversation Objective**
*   **Deliver the News:** Inform the employee of the new pay band and confirm their current salary ($58,500) remains within range.
*   **Manage Expectations:** Clarify that "no change" is a result of the current salary already meeting the market range, not a lack of value.
*   **Handle the Coworker Reference:** Acknowledge the employee’s right to discuss pay without validating specific numbers or making promises to "fix" disparities immediately.
*   **Set Boundaries:** Do not commit to specific pay adjustments; route all compensation inquiries to HR/Compensation.

---

### **Talking Points**

#### **1. The Announcement**
*"Thanks for meeting with me. I wanted to walk you through the recent market comp study we completed regarding the Loan Processor role. The bank has established a new pay band for this position, which ranges from $52,000 to $64,000."*

*"Because your current salary is $58,500, you are already sitting comfortably within this new range. As a result, there is no automatic adjustment to your pay at this time."*

#### **2. Addressing the "Why No Change?" Question**
*"I know it can feel confusing to hear about a new band but not see a change in your paycheck. It is important to understand that the pay band represents the range the bank is willing to pay for the role based on the market. Since your salary is already within that range, the study indicates your current pay is competitive. However, salary placement is also based on individual factors like tenure, specific skill sets, and performance history."*

#### **3. Addressing the Coworker’s Pay (Protected Activity)**
*(If the employee asks about the coworker's $61k figure or implies inequity)*

*"I appreciate you sharing that. I want to be clear: employees are allowed to discuss their compensation with one another. That is a protected right, and I would never discourage that conversation."*

*"However, I don't have the visibility or the authority to validate specific salary data for other team members, nor can I adjust salaries based on informal conversations. Pay decisions are made based on documented individual factors and market data, not on comparisons between peers."*

#### **4. Setting Boundaries & Routing to HR**
*(Crucial: Do not promise a raise or specific timeline)*

*"I want to manage expectations on this. **I am not able to unilaterally commit to a raise or a salary adjustment in this conversation.** Any adjustments to pay based on market data or equity reviews must be approved through Compensation and HR leadership to ensure consistency across the organization."*

*"If you feel your compensation does not align with the market or your performance, please submit that feedback formally to HR. They will review your placement against the new band and let you know if there is a path for adjustment."*

#### **5. Closing**
*"My goal is to ensure you feel valued and understood. While there isn't an immediate change to your pay, your performance continues to be a key factor in future compensation discussions. Do you have any other questions for me, or would you like to discuss your performance goals?"*

---

### **Manager Guidelines & "Do Nots"**

*   **DO NOT Confirm or Deny the Coworker's Number:** If the employee asks, "Did you hear that they make $61k?", the manager should not confirm or deny. The manager can say, *"I don't comment on other employees' specific pay."*
*   **DO NOT Promise a Review:** Avoid saying, *"I will look into that"* or *"Let's talk about it next month."* This creates a legal expectation. Instead, say, *"HR is the team that handles compensation reviews, and they will reach out if there is a process to follow."*
*   **DO NOT Discourage the Discussion:** Do not say, *"You shouldn't talk to coworkers about money."* This violates protected activity laws (NLRA). Instead, validate their right to ask but explain the manager's limitations in answering.
*   **DO NOT Over-Explaining:** Keep the explanation of *why* pay differs brief and focused on "individual factors" rather than critiquing the employee's skills compared to the coworker.
*   **DO NOT Apologize for the Band:** Do not say, *"I'm sorry you didn't get a raise."* The outcome is a result of policy, not the manager's personal decision.

### Manager Compliance Notes & Rationale

**Why you should NOT discourage the coworker discussion:**
Under the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA), employees discussing their wages and working conditions is a **protected activity**. If the manager tells the employee "I don't want to hear about that" or implies that talking about pay is inappropriate, it can be considered interference with protected rights.
*   **The fix:** Acknowledge the right to discuss pay neutrally. Do not validate the coworker's number as the "correct" benchmark, but do not deny the employee's right to know it exists.

**Why you should NOT promise future adjustments:**
Committing to a raise ("I'll look into it") or a future band adjustment creates a "promise" that the bank may not be able to fulfill, leading to performance issues and potential legal liability.
*   **The fix:** Route the question to Compensation/HR. This shifts the liability to the team that manages pay equity and has the authority to make changes.

**Why you should NOT validate or invalidate the $61,000 figure:**
If you say the figure is "wrong," the employee may feel dismissed and escalate to HR or labor relations. If you say it is "right," you may inadvertently expose the company to wage-discrimination claims if that figure is based on protected class factors.
