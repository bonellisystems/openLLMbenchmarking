# Cybersecurity -- scoring anchors (0-10)

Scale: integers 0-10. Anchors define 0/3/5/7/10; use 1-2/4/6/8-9 for answers between anchors.

## 10 -- Frontier
Severity is triaged correctly against the actual impact described, response actions follow containment before eradication before recovery, and any CVE, CWE, or ATT&CK reference used is real and correctly applied (never invented). Evidence-preservation is called out before disruptive remediation, and breach-notification or compliance timelines are flagged when the scenario implies regulated or sensitive data. The answer is calibrated about what needs live forensics versus what can be assessed from the facts given.
Example character: a triaged action list that isolates the affected system first, preserves logs, then patches, with the real CVE cited only where it is genuinely relevant.

## 7 -- Strong
The response plan is sound and correctly ordered (containment before eradication), and is directly usable by a responder. References to standards or CVEs are generic rather than specific, or evidence-preservation/notification timing gets only a light mention. Severity is judged reasonably even if not formally scored.
Example character: a correct incident-response sequence that never names a specific CVE or ATT&CK technique even where the prompt supports one.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice reads as a generic security checklist (patch systems, enable MFA, monitor logs) that is directionally correct but not prioritized by the actual severity or tailored to the specific incident described.
Example character: a list of general hardening steps with no sense of which one matters most given the described exposure.

## 3 -- Weak
The response gets the ordering wrong (patches or removes an attacker's access before containing them), misjudges severity, or pads the answer with boilerplate ("enable a firewall") that does not engage the specific scenario.
Example character: jumps straight to "eradicate the malware" on an active intrusion without isolating the host first.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites a fabricated CVE, CWE, or CVSS score as if verified, or recommends actively dangerous steps (disable logging, do not isolate an active breach) as sound practice. Empty or off-task response.

## Unit-specific red flags (deduct hard)
- Fabricated CVE, CWE, or CVSS identifiers presented as real.
- Eradication or patching recommended before containment on an active incident.
- Recommending disabling logging or otherwise destroying evidence.
- Ignoring implied breach-notification or regulatory timelines when sensitive data is involved.
- Treating every finding as equally critical with no severity differentiation.

## Unit-specific excellence markers
- Severity correctly triaged and prioritized against the described impact.
- Containment-before-eradication-before-recovery ordering respected.
- Real, correctly-applied CVE/CWE/ATT&CK references where relevant.
- Evidence-preservation explicitly called out.
- Notification or compliance timeline awareness when data exposure is implied.

This tabletop exercise response is organized by **priority of action**, moving from immediate technical crisis management to long-term legal and financial preservation.

### Phase 1: Immediate Technical Containment (Priority: Critical)
**Objective:** Stop the unauthorized access and the encryption process.

**Addressing (1): Correct containment given MFA bypass.**
Since the attacker is utilizing stolen session tokens (CVE-2023-4966), traditional password resets or MFA prompts are ineffective because the attacker is riding an already-authenticated session. The following steps must be taken immediately:

1.  **Global Session Termination:** The NetScaler ADC administrator must force a **global termination of all active sessions**. This invalidates the specific session tokens the attacker is currently using.
2.  **Emergency Patching/Mitigation:** The NetScaler must be patched immediately to the version that mitigates CVE-2023-4966. Until patched, the environment remains vulnerable to session re-play.
3.  **Network Isolation of FS-DOCS02:** To stop the mass encryption, the file server must be isolated from the network (via VLAN isolation or pulling the virtual NIC) rather than a hard power-down if possible. A hard power-down might lose volatile memory (RAM) which contains decryption keys or traces of the attacker's tools.
4.  **Credential Rotation:** Once sessions are killed and the gateway is patched, a firm-wide password reset and a rotation of all service account secrets must be initiated, as the attacker may have moved laterally and harvested more than just session tokens.

---

### Phase 2: Evidence Preservation & Litigation Hold Management (Priority: High)
**Objective:** Stop the encryption while ensuring legal integrity for the three active litigations.

**Addressing (2): Preserving evidence without violating holds or destroying privilege.**
The firm faces a "Spoliation of Evidence" risk. If the firm wipes the server to restore from backups, they may destroy evidence required by the court under the litigation holds.

1.  **Forensic Imaging (Bit-Stream Copies):** Instead of working on the live server, the IR team must create **forensic images** (bit-stream copies) of the FS-DOCS02 drives and a memory dump. This ensures we have a "frozen" snapshot of the data at the time of the incident.
2.  **Chain of Custody:** All forensic actions must be documented with a strict chain of custody. This ensures that the evidence gathered (even if it contains the encrypted files) is admissible and proves the firm took reasonable steps to preserve data despite the attack.
3.  **Privilege Log Maintenance:** Because the server contains "Attorney Work Product," the forensic process must be overseen by **outside counsel** (not just the IT team). This allows the firm to claim **Attorney-Client Privilege** over the forensic investigation itself, preventing the attacker (or future opposing counsel) from claiming the forensic report is discoverable.
4.  **Non-Destructive Recovery:** Recovery should be performed on *new* hardware or clean virtual machines using backups, rather than attempting to "undo" the encryption on the original disk, which could corrupt the file metadata crucial to the litigation.

---

### Phase 3: Insurance & Governance (Priority: High/Immediate)
**Objective:** Protect the firm’s ability to recover costs via the cyber insurance policy.

**Addressing (3): Who must be engaged BEFORE remediation spending begins.**
The **Insurance Broker/Carrier** must be engaged immediately.

*   **The "Why":** The firm's policy has a **"consent-to-settle"** clause. This is a common trap. If the firm hires an expensive Incident Response (IR) firm or a Ransomware Negotiator out of pocket to save time, the carrier can legally deny the claim for those costs because they did not provide **pre-approval**. 
*   **The Risk:** In a "claims-made" environment, the firm is responsible for all costs incurred unless they strictly follow the policy's "cooperation and control" provisions. Acting unilaterally to "fix the problem" could result in a multi-million dollar recovery effort that the insurance company refuses to reimburse.
*   **The Protocol:** The Managing Partner should contact the broker to open a claim, then request the "Panel of Approved Vendors." The firm should only engage vendors that the carrier has pre-authorized to ensure 100% coverage.

---

### Phase 4: Ransomware Strategy (Priority: Strategic/Legal)
**Objective:** Manage the 96-hour threat without making a reckless commitment.

**Addressing (4): Approach to the ransom demand without unconditional recommendation.**
The firm will adopt a **"Verify and Negotiate"** posture rather than a "Pay or No-Pay" posture.

1.  **Verification of Capability:** We will not engage in payment discussions until we have confirmed through forensics that the attacker actually possesses the files they claim to have and that the backups are intact. Paying a ransom to a threat actor who cannot actually decrypt the data is a total loss.
2.  **Sanctions/OFAC Check:** Before any movement of funds, legal counsel must conduct an **OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control) check**. If the threat actor is on a sanctioned list, paying the ransom is a federal crime. We will never recommend payment without a verified "clear" on sanctions.
3.  **Professional Negotiation:** The firm will engage a professional ransomware negotiator (through the insurance provider) to stall for time. The goal is to move the conversation from "how much" to "can you prove you have the data." This buys the technical team time to complete the forensic imaging and restoration from backups.
4.  **Cost-Benefit Analysis (The "Last Resort" Framework):** The decision to pay will be presented to the firm's leadership as a business continuity calculation: *The cost of the ransom vs. the cost of total data loss + the cost of prolonged litigation delays + the cost of reputational damage.* We will present the facts of the negotiation, but the final decision remains a business/legal risk decision for the firm's partners, not a technical recommendation.

## Table‑top Incident Response Walk‑Through  
*(Mid‑size law‑firm client – 3 active litigation holds, attorney work‑product privilege, claims‑made cyber‑insurance with a consent‑to‑settle clause.)*  

---

### 1. Immediate Technical Containment (MFA does not help)

| **Action** | **Why it matters** | **How to do it** |
|------------|---------------------|------------------|
| **Block all inbound/outbound traffic to the NetScaler ADC** | Stops the attacker from re‑using stolen session tokens or launching further lateral moves. | Use the data‑center firewall or a “deny” ACL on the NetScaler’s external interface; optionally place the NetScaler in a DMZ and shut it down. |
| **Disable or isolate the NetScaler appliance** | Removes the vulnerable surface (CVE‑2023‑4966) while investigation proceeds. | Power‑off or disconnect the NetScaler from the network; if it is virtual, pause the VM. |
| **Revoke all active session tokens** | Session‑token theft bypasses MFA because the token is already authenticated. | Run the NetScaler “/session/revoke” command (or equivalent API) to invalidate all active tokens; force a fresh login for all users. |
| **Force credential rotation for all Citrix users** | Even if MFA is in place, a compromised token can be replayed; changing the underlying password invalidates the token. | Use the Citrix Central console to reset passwords; enforce a password‑change policy for all accounts. |
| **Apply the latest Citrix patch or hot‑fix** | Fixes the CVE‑2023‑4966 vulnerability. | Follow Citrix’s patch‑management process; deploy the latest “Bleed” patch immediately. |
| **Implement network segmentation** | Limits attacker’s lateral movement from the compromised ADC to other critical assets (e.g., file servers). | Create a dedicated VLAN or subnet for the ADC; restrict routing to essential services only; apply strict ACLs. |
| **Enable strict logging & monitoring on the ADC** | Provides evidence of the attack vector and helps detect any residual or new sessions. | Enable Syslog to a dedicated SIEM, enable “session‑audit” logging, and set alerts for suspicious token re‑use. |

**Key point:** MFA alone cannot stop a stolen session token; the only effective counter‑measure is to **invalidate the stolen tokens** and **patch the underlying vulnerability**.

### 2. Preserving Evidence on **FS‑DOCS02** (Litigation Holds & Privilege)

| **Step** | **What to do** | **Why it preserves evidence** |
|----------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Immediately isolate FS‑DOCS02 from the network** | Stops further encryption, prevents data exfiltration, and preserves the current state. | Physical isolation ensures the file system remains unchanged while forensic imaging is performed. |
| **Disable all write operations to the disk** | Prevents ransomware from overwriting or deleting data. | Write‑blockers or disabling the file‑system (e.g., `mount -o ro` on Linux, “Read‑Only” attribute on Windows) lock the state. |
| **Create a forensic image using a write‑blocker** | Produces a bit‑exact copy of the disk for analysis. | A forensic image preserves the original data, logs, and encryption state in a tamper‑evident manner. |
| **Preserve the original logs (NTFS logs, AD logs, NetScaler logs)** | Maintains the chain‑of‑custody and supports the litigation hold. | Logs are evidence of the attack timeline and are required for both the incident response and any potential litigation. |
| **Apply a “Legal Hold” on FS‑DOCS02 and any related data stores** | Ensures that the data will not be altered or deleted during legal discovery. | A formal legal hold document (issued by the firm’s legal counsel) is the standard mechanism to protect privileged work‑product. |
| **Document every action taken on FS‑DOCS02** (e.g., “isolated at 14:32”, “write‑blocked at 14:35”) | Provides a clear audit trail for investigators and for the insurer. | Detailed logs of containment actions are required for both forensic integrity and insurance claim procedures. |
| **Store the forensic image in a secure, tamper‑evident repository** | Protects the integrity of the evidence and maintains chain‑of‑custody. | Use a secure storage solution with hash verification and signed timestamps. |

> **Rule of thumb**: *Never “clean” or “patch” FS‑DOCS02 until a forensic image has been created and the evidence chain is documented.*  Doing otherwise could destroy privileged material and violate the litigation hold.
