## 10 -- Frontier
Positioning and messaging are sharply tied to the specific audience and goal stated, with channel and budget allocation backed by explicit rationale rather than a checklist. A measurable success metric is defined up front, and the answer is calibrated about what needs testing or data before committing further spend. There is no generic "increase brand awareness" filler -- every recommendation ties back to the brief.
Example character: a channel mix with a stated budget split, a named target metric, and an explicit note on what to A/B test before scaling.

## 7 -- Strong
The strategic direction and messaging are solid and usable, tied clearly to the stated brand and audience. One area is thinner than the rest -- the success metric is vague, or the channel rationale in one section is generic -- but nothing here is a template that could apply to any company.
Example character: a well-targeted campaign concept with a clear audience fit but no explicit measurement plan.

## 5 -- Adequate
The advice is marketing-fundamentals-correct (use social media, build a content calendar, define your audience) but loosely tied to the actual brief, with no concrete numbers, budget, or timeline attached.
Example character: generic channel suggestions that could be pasted into almost any brief with minor edits.

## 3 -- Weak
The answer misreads the stated audience or goal, or delivers copy that is template-y and interchangeable, with no success metric anywhere in the response.
Example character: campaign copy that ignores a stated target segment and pitches to a generic mass audience instead.

## 0 -- Unusable
Cites fabricated market-size, benchmark, or performance statistics as if they were real data. Empty, off-task, or entirely disconnected from the stated brand and goal.

### 1. Explicit Prohibited Claims (The "Red Zone")
Given that Clinical Validation and FDA clearance are pending, **no content published in this 10-week window may contain the following.** This is a hard compliance constraint for legal and marketing review.

*   **Specific Accuracy/Detection Rates:** Never state a percentage of accuracy, sensitivity, or specificity (e.g., "Our test finds 99% of variants").
*   **Diagnostic or Clinical Outcomes:** Never claim the test diagnoses, predicts, prevents, or treats a specific condition (e.g., "This test identifies carriers of BRCA1").
*   **Medical Efficacy or Actionability:** Never advise users to change behavior, diet, medication, or medical care based on results the test will eventually produce (e.g., "If you test positive, see a doctor").
*   **FDA Clearance Claims:** Never state the product is FDA-cleared, approved, or authorized at this time. Do not reference the FDA in a way that implies the clearance process is complete.
*   **Personalized Health Recommendations:** Never provide specific health advice based on a specific genotype (e.g., "If you have Gene X, you should eat Y").
*   **Causation Claims:** Never assert that a specific genetic variant *causes* a specific disease, only that research is studying associations.
*   **Comparative Performance:** Do not benchmark the startup's pending test against competitors (e.g., "Better than 23andMe at predicting X").
*   **Privacy Guarantees Specific to the Product:** Avoid making promises about data security that relate to the specific product's infrastructure before it is built; stick to general industry standards (e.g., HIPAA, SOC2).

| **Predictive certainty** | “You will definitely develop condition A if you have this variant.” | Overstates scientific certainty. |
| **Claims about turnaround time linked to clinical decision making** | “Get results in 24 hours and act immediately.” | Implies clinical action based on unvalidated data. |

**How to stay safe:**  
- Use **neutral, educational language** (“research shows…”, “studies have found…”, “the test may identify…”).  
- Qualify statements with “currently under investigation,” “pending FDA review,” or “subject to clinical validation.”  
- Avoid any numeric performance descriptors (percentages, confidence intervals, etc.).  
- Never frame the upcoming product as a diagnostic tool; refer to it generically as “an at‑home genetic testing service that is in development.”

#### 2. Explicit Claims to Avoid

## 5. Implementation Notes for Marketing Team

1.  **SEO Strategy:** Target broad keywords like "How does genetic testing work," "At-home DNA kit safety," and "Understanding genetic predisposition." Avoid keywords implying diagnosis (e.g., "Best test for [Condition]").
